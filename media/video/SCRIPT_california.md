## BEAT 1 — Cold open: 1908 (0:00–0:55)
[B1: clipping board — LA Herald 27 May 1908 headline; slow push-in]

**NARRATION:** In the spring of 1908, in the canyons of southern Orange County, government men
ordered cattle into a bath. Several hundred head were dipped at the ranch of J. C. Joplin in
Trabuco Canyon. Six or seven hundred more at San Juan Capistrano. The solution was the federal
formula: eight pounds of white arsenic to five hundred gallons of water.

[B2: clipping board — "KILLED TICKS AND CATTLE," SJ Mercury 26 Jun 1908]

Weeks later, a dairyman named Levengood — he'd bought his herd from Joplin's Bell Canyon ranch —
watched six of his cattle die. The newspapers printed the cause: *the arsenic preparation
recommended by the Government.* He billed the county four hundred dollars.

That dip happened. The deaths happened. The claim happened. All of it is in the primary record.

## BEAT 2 — The program (0:55–1:55)
[B3: 1912 quarantine-district map plate; B4: USDA warning-sign reproduction]

**NARRATION:** This wasn't one ranch's experiment. In 1907, California made tick-dipping
compulsory in quarantined counties — part of a national campaign against Texas cattle fever.
Orange County was on the federal list from the beginning, and stayed there until March 1912, when
the quarantine was lifted and the dipping stopped.

The USDA even printed the warning that hung beside every vat: *POISONOUS to man and all animals…
do not allow it to contaminate any feed or water supply.* The danger was never a secret. It was
typeset.

By our model, California's program ran on the order of fifty to a hundred and fifty vats
statewide. [MODEL ESTIMATE badge]

## BEAT 3 — The chemistry (1:55–2:40)
[B5: two-arsenics explainer board, part 1 — the element]

**NARRATION:** Arsenic is not a chemical that breaks down. It's an element — it has nothing to
break down *into*. It binds to soil and stays. Around a single heavily-used vat, the mass balance
works out to something like one to five hundred pounds of arsenic in the ground — a model
estimate, not a measurement, because in California, no one has ever measured. [MODEL ESTIMATE badge]

## BEAT 4 — The contrast: what everyone else did (2:40–4:00)
[B6: FL-vs-CA process comparison board — two columns animating]

**NARRATION:** Here's the part of the story that belongs to California specifically.

In Florida and across the Southeast, tick eradication was a *government operation*. State crews
built the vats, ran the dippings — and wrote the locations down. Florida can point to more than
three thousand former vat sites today. In New South Wales, Australia, the government went further:
a register of dip sites, official guidelines, and notice to anyone buying land near one.

California did it differently. The state mandated the dipping — and left the building and running
of vats to each ranch, working from a mailed federal circular. The USDA's own bulletin said it
plainly: *homemade dips are the ones most commonly used.* No agency recorded where they were. When
the quarantine lifted in 1912, the locations left the public record with it.

One state wrote it down. One state didn't. That single clerical difference is this whole story.

## BEAT 5 — The decades of silence (4:00–5:00)
[B7: timeline board 1906→2026; the long empty middle]

**NARRATION:** Then came the silence. No follow-up in the thirties, when the ranches changed
hands. None in the fifties. None under the environmental laws of the seventies — because the tools
those laws created look backward through aerial photography, and the photographs begin around
1950. Dipping ended in 1912. The vats were invisible to every review that would ever be run.

When the master-planned communities rose on the old ranges — the sixties through the two-thousands
— the reviews came back clean. Not because anyone tested the soil. Because the hazard had never
been written where a review would look.

## BEAT 6 — The areas (5:00–6:20)
[B8: CA probability map — the user-specified centerpiece; then Ladera time-series; then Joplin patent overlay]

**NARRATION:** Our probability map grades nineteen former ranch systems, from *documented* to
*plausible* — with today's communities named on each. [map holds on screen]

Ladera Ranch: built on cattle range inside the quarantine county. Its environmental reviews — a
1995 assessment and a 2003 school review — were records-and-walk exercises. No soil was sampled.
Arsenic was never an analyte. Never looked — which is not the same as looked-and-cleared.

Coto de Caza: the one *documented* dip ranch. We located Joplin's homestead patent to the survey
section — part of it now under northeast Coto's homes and golf course, while the old home place in
Bell Canyon, where the working corrals stood, remains open canyon to this day.

And in Coto there's a control case. When developers found a *documented* hazard on one tract — an
old shotgun range — the system worked perfectly: soil tested, cleanup plan, supervised closure.
Lead and clay-target residue, found and handled. The dip, undocumented, never triggered anything.
Same ground. Same rules. The difference was a record.

## BEAT 7 — The wrong arsenic (6:20–7:00)
[B5 part 2: lead-arsenate (As+Pb) vs arsenic trioxide (As alone)]

**NARRATION:** Where California *has* tested soil arsenic — mostly old orchard land under school
guidelines — it tested for the farm pesticide: lead-arsenate, arsenic traveling *with* lead. The
cattle-dip poison is different: arsenic trioxide — arsenic with little or no lead. That signature
is exactly what a lab can distinguish. And it has never been the target of any California test.

## BEAT 8 — The resolver (7:00–7:45)
[B9: the undisturbed Bell Canyon ground, slow aerial pan; end cards]

**NARRATION:** The reported pattern warrants investigation — and the available evidence does not
yet establish causation. Both of those sentences are true at once. What separates them is a soil
test: total arsenic, lead, and speciation, on documented ground. A century of missing paperwork
comes down to a few kilograms of dirt.

The knowledge was never missing. Only the marking was. The question is whether California finally
writes it down.
