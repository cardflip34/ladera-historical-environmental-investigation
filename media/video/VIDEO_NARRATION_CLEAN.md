A few days ago, I had no intention of making this video. But after everything I've
discovered, I knew the story needed to be told, and hopefully heard.

A little over 100 years ago, in 1907, California ordered its ranchers, not only here in
Orange County but across the state, to dip their cattle in arsenic. By 1908 it was
mandated. It was happening on ranch after ranch, across the county and the state. The
whole country was battling the Texas fever tick, a parasite that spread disease and was
killing cattle nationwide. It became a national campaign to wipe it out, and the federal
government even supplied the formula for the dip, with the arsenic in it.

But it was California that mandated its own ranchers to build the vats, gave them the
instructions and the formula, and then left it up to them to document and record where
those locations were. Unfortunately, it doesn't seem like that happened. Something slipped
through the cracks.

And this was not a small amount of poison. It was a fixed formula from the federal
government, poured season after season into the same ground that would ultimately become
some of Southern California's most sought-after communities. And California never went back
to check.

It was mandatory. Every detail of how to do it was written down. But where it was done was
never recorded. And when the program closed, these locations quietly slipped out of history.

My name is Andy Stavros. I'm not a scientist, I'm not a doctor, I'm not an activist. I'm a
father here in Newport Beach. And before I say anything else, my heart goes out to the
families, the children, the parents, everyone affected by this. As a father myself, I can't
imagine what you're going through.

And like a lot of parents, my kids are always outside. Parks, soccer fields, bike trails,
greenbelts. Not just here in Newport Beach, but everywhere in Orange County. And like most
parents, I assumed those places were cared for in similar ways, by similar landscape
maintenance companies, using the same herbicides and pesticides. So it just didn't make
sense to me.

When I first heard about the reported Ewing sarcoma cases in Ladera Ranch, my first thought
was not "it has to be pesticides." Honestly, it was the opposite. If the same kinds of
products are used across Orange County, why would one community be different? Why Ladera
Ranch? Why not Newport Beach? Why not Irvine? Why not Mission Viejo? That question stayed
with me. And when something doesn't make sense to me, I have a hard time letting it go until
I understand it.

I was nervous about releasing this. Not because I think I found all the answers, I didn't,
but because of the ripple effects. Families worry, communities worry, people worry about
their children, their schools, the place they call home.

But I kept coming back to one question. If this was my neighborhood, and these were my
parks, my own kids, where they played, would I want to know? 100%. Not because history
proves there's a problem, but because as a parent, I'd want every reasonable question
explored as thoroughly as possible.

So that became the purpose. Not to prove a theory, not to accuse anyone, not to tell anyone
what to believe. Just to ask: have we looked at everything?

What started as a few hours over the weekend turned into a three-day investigation, squeezed
in around summer life with kids and work. And a few days later, here we are. The more I dug
into Ladera Ranch, the faster I realized this was bigger than Ladera Ranch. It was bigger
than me. What I had pulled on was not a story about one neighborhood, or one family, or one
theory of mine. It was a thread of California's history that reached far beyond the place
where it started.

I used modern AI the way I believe investigators and journalists increasingly will. Not to
generate answers, but to organize enormous amounts of public information, search historical
metadata, compare disconnected archives, and surface records that would be almost impossible
for one person to assemble alone. Then I verified everything, with AI and with my own eyes.
Century-old newspapers, government reports, historical aerial photography, land patents,
environmental reviews, old maps, ranch photographs.

Almost none of it was hidden. It had been public for decades. The problem was that the
pieces had never been assembled into one investigation. And when I started assembling them,
this stopped being a story about pesticides and became a story about the land itself. The
land beneath Ladera Ranch.

Long before the homes and the schools, this was cattle country. Part of the historic O'Neill
Ranch, one of the great cattle operations of Southern California. The same hills where families live and children play today were once open range. And that is where it turns. Because this was cattle country, it sat squarely inside the arsenic dipping program. And this is not a myth, and it's not a rumor. It's written in the
government's own records. The US Department of Agriculture printed the entire program. The
formula, eight pounds of arsenic to five hundred gallons of water. The engineering drawings
for the vats. Even the poison warnings meant to hang on every single one. That's how
dangerous it was.

Here's why that matters. Arsenic is an element. It does not break down, it does not degrade.
It binds to soil, and it stays. So if one of those vats operated somewhere, its arsenic is,
in principle, still in the ground.

And the one thing I could not find anywhere was a record of where, on these ranches, the
vats were actually built. The government documented how to build them, and what to put in
them, but never documented where. The most recent aerial photography I could find, using
metadata, was from around 1929. You can see the old ranches, but by then the vats had
already been gone since 1912.

Then a single newspaper page changed everything. In 1908, a paper described cattle being
dipped at the ranch of J.C. Joplin, in Trabuco Canyon, on the same ranch lands I'd been
mapping. From that one name, I followed his federal land patent and the original survey
sections until I could place that ranch on today's map. For the first time, I was not just
reading that California dipped cattle in arsenic. I could point to the ground where
documented dipping actually happened.

In the Southeast, and in Florida, this same program was run by the government. State crews
logged the locations. They knew where everything was. Florida can still tell you where
thousands of those sites are today. Australia went even further. It keeps public records of
these locations, so anyone buying that land is told what lies beneath it. California did it
differently. The state ordered the dipping, then left the building and running of the vats
to each individual rancher. No agency kept a list. When the quarantine ended in 1912 and the
cattle moved on, the locations left with them.

So I tried to rebuild what was lost. Using aerial photographs from the 1920s, '30s, and
'40s, I laid the old ranch landscape back over the neighborhoods that exist today. I traced
the roads, the stock ponds, the corrals, the drainage, and the property lines. I followed
land patents and survey sections, and compared century-old ranch photos to modern satellite
views. Some of what I found strengthened the questions, some of it weakened them. Both
belonged in the report. One feature I first believed might be a ranch site became far less
convincing once I found sharper imagery, and I kept it in, because a real investigation
preserves the evidence that argues against it, not only the evidence that agrees.

And that led to the question at the center of all this. The environmental reviews I examined
for Ladera Ranch used the standard tools of their time. Records checks, site visits, aerial
photos. But those all dated back to the '50s, nothing before. The arsenic dipping was
decades before that, so nothing was on there. There was no testing for this type of arsenic
in the soil, because they didn't know to test for it. In fact, I found aerial and satellite
images that weren't discovered during the time of the phase one reports for the entire
development. They weren't in there. This entire time period, this blind spot, was missed. In
the public materials I could find, I saw no sign that this specific early ranching history
was ever reconstructed or evaluated. It simply came before the window anyone was looking
through.

There's one more distinction that decides almost everything, and it's easy to miss. There
are two different kinds of arsenic in this story. The first is lead arsenate. It was a farm
pesticide, sprayed for decades on orchards, and it leaves a telltale fingerprint: arsenic
bound together with lead. The second is arsenic trioxide. That was the cattle-dip poison,
and it leaves the opposite fingerprint: arsenic with little or no lead.

That difference is everything, because of where the testing has, and has not, happened. In
the handful of places where California did test local soil for arsenic, including at some
school sites built on former orchards, it was looking for the pesticide. The lead-arsenate
signature. As far as I could tell, no test in these communities has ever gone looking for
the other one. The dipping signature. The arsenic trioxide. The schools, the parks, and the
homes built on the old ranch and grazing land were never checked for it. So it has never
been ruled in, and it has never been ruled out.

Now let me be as clear as I possibly can about what this is not. None of this establishes
contamination. None of it establishes exposure. And none of it establishes that anything
caused anyone's illness. This report does not conclude that arsenic caused Ewing sarcoma.
The published science I reviewed does not link arsenic to that particular cancer, and I say
so plainly in the report. This was never about forcing a connection. If anything, I tried to
disconnect it every which way I could. It does not accuse any developer, any agency, any
landowner of wrongdoing. And the other possible explanations, including pesticides, stay
firmly on the table.

What this is, is a historical reconstruction, built entirely from public records, maps,
photographs, and government documents, and the honest questions that reconstruction raises.
And I've published all of it. Every map, every overlay, every newspaper page, every
government document, every photograph, every citation. Not so people will take my word for
it. The opposite. I hope people read it, challenge it, verify it, and make it better.
Because if this investigation taught me anything, it's that history doesn't disappear.
Sometimes it simply waits for someone to connect the dots. And this is my attempt to do
exactly that. I hope you'll read it for yourself.

Honestly, I was hoping to be wrong. I looked for ways to be wrong.

Ultimately, the locations of the vats need to be identified, in order to understand where
the soil should be tested. Because this is bigger than Ladera Ranch. This is statewide. I've
overlaid the potential hotspots across Southern California, and where they might fall over
existing communities.

I've done everything I could, to the best of my ability, at this point. There are archives
around Orange County with non-digitized documents, photographs, and manager logs. Anything
to help narrow this down would be a tremendous help. I'm happy to stay involved. I pray for
all the families, the communities, everybody's health, and every safety. I don't have the
answers. I had a lot of questions, and I've done everything I could to answer what I could.
And I think this needs larger attention.

Because from what I've seen, the past could uncover a lot, and provide answers we're looking
for today. Thanks for listening. I created a website with the full report. Every picture,
every overlay, every satellite image, everything I have. If you have anything that might
help this investigation, I'm happy to stay involved. I'll provide contact information and
the website.
