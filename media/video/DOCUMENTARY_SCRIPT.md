# The Arsenic Under the Master Plan: narration script (finalized)

**Production notes**
- Finalized first-person narration in Andy Stavros's voice. Combines the personal documentary cut
  with the factual investigation spine.
- ~1,200 words, roughly 8 minutes at a natural documentary pace.
- The introduction stays full and human (~500 words), but the film opens **cold on arsenic** in the
  first ten seconds, and the core discovery (the land under Ladera Ranch and the government's
  1907-1908 arsenic-dipping mandate) lands **within the first ~90 seconds**, not five minutes in,
  the way the earlier draft did.
- Discipline: hypothesis-neutral. No causation, no accusation. The honest science, that arsenic is
  not linked to Ewing sarcoma, is stated plainly. Every claim is sourced in the report. No em dashes.
- No beats and no image cues here by design. Once this script is locked, we map the visuals to it
  (the USDA report, the Ladera aerials, the Joplin newspaper and land-patent overlay, the
  Florida-versus-California contrast, the two-arsenics graphic).

---

A little over a hundred years ago, in 1907, California ordered ranchers, here in Orange County and
across the state, to dip their cattle in arsenic.

By 1908, it was happening on ranch after ranch across this county. The whole country was fighting the
Texas fever tick, a parasite that spread a disease deadly to cattle and was draining the American
livestock industry of millions. Wiping it out became a national campaign, and the federal government
supplied the formula for the dip. But it was California that made the dipping mandatory for its own
ranchers, and then left it to each of them to build the vats, to run them, and to record them. No
state agency ever compiled where they were.

The program ran until 1912. Then the quarantine was lifted, the cattle moved on, and the vats were
left behind. This was not a small amount of poison. It was mixed to a federal formula and poured,
season after season, into the same ground that would one day become some of Southern California's
most sought-after neighborhoods. And in the decades that followed, California never went back to
check.

It was mandatory. Every detail of how to do it was written down. Where it was done never was. And when
the program ended, those locations quietly slipped out of history.

My name is Andy Stavros. I'm not a scientist. I'm not a doctor. I'm not an activist. I'm a father in
Newport Beach.

Before I say anything else, I want to speak to the families. My heart goes out to every child, every
parent, and everyone affected by the reported pediatric cancer cases in Ladera Ranch. You have been
in my thoughts throughout this entire investigation. As a father myself, I cannot imagine what you
have been through.

Like a lot of parents, my kids are always outside. Parks. Soccer fields. Bike trails and greenbelts.
Not just where we live in Newport Beach, but all over Orange County. And like most parents, I assumed
most of those places are cared for in similar ways. Similar landscaping. Similar fertilizers. Similar
herbicides and pesticides.

So when I first heard about the reported Ewing sarcoma cases in Ladera Ranch, my first thought was
not, "it has to be pesticides." Honestly, it was the opposite. If the same kinds of products are used
all across Orange County, why would one community be different? Why Ladera Ranch? Why not Newport
Beach? Why not Irvine? Why not Mission Viejo?

That question stayed with me. It didn't make sense. And when something doesn't make sense, I have a
hard time letting it go until I understand it.

I'll be honest about something else. I was nervous about releasing this. Not because I think I found
all the answers. I didn't. But because information like this has ripple effects. Families worry.
Communities worry. People worry about their children, their schools, and the place they call home.

But I kept coming back to one question. If this were my neighborhood, and these were the parks where
my own kids played, and someone had uncovered a forgotten chapter of the land's history, would I want
to know? For me, the answer was yes. Not because history proves there is a problem, but because as a
parent I would want every reasonable question explored as thoroughly as possible.

So that became the purpose. Not to prove a theory. Not to accuse anyone. Not to tell anyone what to
believe. Just to ask one thing. Have we looked at everything?

What started as a few hours over a weekend turned into a three-day investigation, squeezed in around
summer life with the kids and work. And a few days later, here we are. The more I dug into Ladera
Ranch, the faster I realized this was bigger than Ladera Ranch. It was bigger than me. What I had
pulled on was not a story about one neighborhood, or one family, or one theory of mine. It was a
thread of California's history that reached far beyond the place where I started.

I used modern AI the
way I believe investigators and journalists increasingly will: not to generate answers, but to
organize enormous amounts of public information, search historical metadata, compare disconnected
archives, and surface records that would be almost impossible for one person to assemble alone. Then
I verified everything by hand. Century-old newspapers. Government reports. Historic aerial
photography. Land patents. Environmental reviews. Old maps and ranch photographs.

Almost none of it was hidden. It had been public for decades. The problem was never access. The
problem was that the pieces had never been assembled into one investigation.

And when I started assembling them, this stopped being a story about pesticides. It became a story
about the land itself. The land beneath Ladera Ranch.

Long before the homes and the schools, this was cattle country. Part of the historic O'Neill Ranch,
and what became Rancho Mission Viejo, one of the great cattle operations in Southern California. The
same hills where families live and children play today were once an open range.

And that is where it turns. Because this was cattle country, it sat squarely inside that
arsenic-dipping program. And this is not folklore, and it is not rumor. It is written in the
government's own records. The U.S. Department of Agriculture printed the entire program. The formula,
eight pounds of white arsenic to five hundred gallons of water. The engineering drawings for the
vats. Even the poison warnings meant to hang on every one.

Here is why that matters. Arsenic is an element. It does not break down. It binds to soil, and it
stays. So if one of those vats operated somewhere, its arsenic is, in principle, still in the ground.

And there was one thing I could not find anywhere. A record of where, on these ranches, the vats were
actually built. The government documented how. It never documented where.

Then a single newspaper page changed everything.

In 1908, a paper described cattle being dipped at the ranch of J. C. Joplin, in Trabuco Canyon, on
the same ranch lands I had been mapping. From that one name, I followed his federal land patent and
the original survey sections until I could place that ranch on today's map. For the first time, I was
not just reading that California dipped cattle in arsenic. I could point to the ground where a
documented dipping actually happened.

And this is the part I believe matters most.

In the Southeast, and in Florida, this same program was run by the government. State crews built the
vats, ran the dippings, and logged the locations. Florida can still tell you where thousands of those
sites are today. Australia went even further, and keeps a public register, so anyone buying that land
is told what lies beneath it.

California did it differently. The state ordered the dipping, then left the building and running of
the vats to each individual rancher, working from a mailed pamphlet. No agency kept the list. When
the quarantine ended in 1912 and the cattle moved on, the locations left with them.

So I tried to rebuild what was lost. Using aerial photographs from the 1920s, 30s, and 40s, I laid
the old ranch landscape back over the neighborhoods we know now. I traced the roads, the stock ponds,
the corrals, the drainage, and the property lines. I followed land patents and survey sections, and
compared century-old ranch photos to modern satellite views.

Some of what I found strengthened the questions. Some of it weakened them. Both belonged in the
report. One feature I first believed might be a ranch site became far less convincing once I found
sharper imagery, and I kept it in, because a real investigation preserves the evidence that argues
against it, not only the evidence that agrees.

And that led to the question at the center of all of this. The environmental reviews I examined for
Ladera Ranch used the standard tools of their time. Records checks. Site visits. Aerial photos. But
the arsenic-dipping era ended decades before the earliest imagery those reviews typically rely on. In
the public materials I could find, I saw no sign that this specific early ranching history was ever
reconstructed or evaluated. It was not hidden. It simply came before the window everyone was looking
through.

There is one more distinction that decides almost everything, and it is easy to miss. There are two
very different kinds of arsenic in this story. The first is lead arsenate. It was a farm pesticide,
sprayed for decades on orchards, and it leaves a telltale fingerprint: arsenic bound together with
lead. The second is arsenic trioxide. That was the cattle-dip poison, and it leaves the opposite
fingerprint: arsenic with little or no lead.

That difference is everything, because of where the testing has, and has not, happened. In the
handful of places where California did test local soil for arsenic, including at some school sites
that were built on former orchards, it was looking for the pesticide. The lead-arsenate signature. As
far as I could find, no test in these communities has ever gone looking for the other one. The
dipping signature. The arsenic trioxide. The schools, the parks, and the homes built on the old
ranch and grazing land were never checked for it. So it has never been ruled in, and it has never
been ruled out.

Now let me be as clear as I possibly can about what this is not. None of this establishes
contamination. None of it establishes exposure. And none of it establishes that anything caused
anyone's illness. This report does not conclude that arsenic caused Ewing sarcoma. The published
science I reviewed does not link arsenic to that particular cancer, and I say so plainly in the
report. This was never about forcing a connection, and it does not accuse any developer, agency, or
landowner of wrongdoing. The other possible explanations, including pesticides, stay firmly on the
table.

What this is, is a historical reconstruction, built entirely from public records, maps, photographs,
and government documents, and the honest questions that reconstruction raises.

I've published all of it. Every map. Every overlay. Every newspaper page. Every government document.
Every photograph. Every citation.

Not so people will take my word for it. The opposite. I hope people read it, challenge it, verify it,
and make it better.

Because if this investigation taught me anything, it is that history doesn't disappear. Sometimes it
simply waits for someone to connect the pieces.

This is my attempt to do exactly that. And I hope you'll read it for yourself.
