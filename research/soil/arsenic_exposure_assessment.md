# Arsenic in Residential Soil — Persistence, Pathways, and Comparison to Pesticides

**Prepared:** 2026-07-18. Hypothesis-neutral. Prompted by a specific and well-posed question:
*Ladera Ranch is fully developed — grass, turf, concrete, asphalt. If arsenic is the concern
rather than pesticides, does the surface even matter, given that grass and backyard produce grow
in that same soil?*

The short answer is that the surface matters **a great deal, but not completely** — and the
direction of the effect is **opposite** for arsenic and for turf herbicides. That asymmetry is
the most useful thing in this document. A second finding, in §6, matters more.

---

## 1. Arsenic's "lifespan": there isn't one

Arsenic is **element 33**. It is not a molecule that breaks down into something else. There is no
half-life, no degradation pathway, no expiry. Soil arsenic from lead-arsenate orchard spraying
applied in the 1920s is still arsenic today, and will still be arsenic in 2126.

What *does* change over time is **speciation and mobility** — whether it is present as arsenite
(As III) or arsenate (As V), how tightly it is bound to iron and aluminium oxides, and therefore
how much of it is actually available for uptake or absorption. These shifts change the
*bioavailable fraction*; they do not remove the arsenic.

This is the fundamental contrast with the pesticides in this investigation:

| | Persistence | Exposure pattern |
|---|---|---|
| **Arsenic** | Permanent — cannot degrade | Continuous, low-level, for as long as one lives there |
| **Glufosinate** | Soil DT50 ≈ **7.4 days** | Pulses at each application, decaying between |
| **Glyphosate** | Soil half-life ≈ **17 days** (typical) | Pulses, decaying between |
| **2,4-D** | Soil half-life ≈ **4–29 days** | Pulses, decaying between |
| **Bifenthrin** | ≈ **87–102 days**, strongly soil-bound | Longer pulses, low mobility |

*(Pesticide fate values: PPDB, source `SRC-PEST-PPDB`.)*

So the two hazards produce fundamentally different dose profiles: **a permanent low background
versus repeated spikes.** Neither pattern is automatically "worse" — that depends on the toxicology,
which is where §6 becomes decisive.

> **On the "half-life of arsenic in soil" figures in circulation.** You will see "6.5 years
> (arsenic trioxide) to 16 years (lead arsenate)" quoted widely. Traced to source (Eisler, USFWS
> Contaminant Hazard Review No. 12, citing NRCC 1978), these describe **loss of the applied
> compound from the surface layer** via leaching, volatilisation and transformation — **not
> destruction of arsenic**. They contradict ATSDR's plain statement and should not be presented as
> decay half-lives. ATSDR: *"Arsenic cannot be destroyed in the environment. It can only change
> its form."* And, decisively for this project: *"Arsenic is largely immobile in agricultural
> soils, therefore, it tends to concentrate and remain in upper soil layers indefinitely."*
> (ATSDR ToxGuide for Arsenic, 2007 — grade A1.)

## 2. Lead arsenate — and an important land-use mismatch

Lead arsenate was the dominant orchard insecticide from roughly the **1890s to the 1950s–60s**,
before DDT displaced it. It leaves behind **both lead and arsenic**. California DTSC names the
contaminants of concern on former orchards as exactly **arsenic, lead, and organochlorine
pesticides** — the same suite found at the former-farm school sites near Ladera Ranch.

**But the crop history does not match the classic lead-arsenate profile, and this matters.**
Lead arsenate's documented heavy use was on **apples and pears**, for codling moth control — the
basis of Washington State's 115,000-acre former-orchard programme. Ladera Ranch sits on former
**Rancho Mission Viejo** land: cattle ranching, dry-farmed barley, and **citrus/lemon** with tree
nurseries. No documentation of lead-arsenate use on **California citrus or avocado** was located.

**UPDATED 2026-07-18 — see `arsenic_historical_use.md` for the full reconstruction.** Two other
mechanisms fit this land considerably better, and one was compulsory:

1. **Arsenical cattle dips (1907–1917).** California mandated disinfection of cattle by arsenical
   dip under a **March 1907 law**; Orange County was among the *last* counties under tick quarantine
   (still quarantined April 1910; programme ended June 1917). That window sits entirely inside
   Jerome O'Neill's management of the cattle operation (1907–1926) on what was Southern California's
   largest such ranch. Dip-vat sites elsewhere measure **300–1,400 mg/kg arsenic** against a regional
   background of ~12 mg/kg.
2. **Sodium arsenite herbicide / soil sterilant (~1906–1960)** — the standard herbicide of commerce
   for that period, applied for soil sterilisation at **400–800 lbs/acre**.

California citrus, meanwhile, was managed for red scale with **cyanide fumigation and oil sprays,
not arsenicals** — confirming the crop mismatch.

**Consequence for sampling design.** The expected pattern is **point-source, not blanket**: intense
hot spots where cattle were worked and chemicals mixed, elevated strips where vegetation was
deliberately killed, and open grazing land near background. A uniform grid survey would likely
*dilute a hot spot into the mean and miss it.* Locate historic corrals, water points and vat
structures from the aerial series first, then sample those.

### Depth — common to all three mechanisms

Whichever route applied, surface-applied arsenical residue concentrates in the **plough layer /
topsoil**, roughly the top 6–18 inches, because it has limited downward mobility.

That depth figure collides with a routine construction fact: standard grading practice **strips
6–12 inches of topsoil** before placing structural fill, because organic topsoil cannot be used
as compacted structural fill. **The strip depth and the contaminated depth are the same horizon.**

Where that stripped material went is therefore consequential, and there are three very different
possibilities:

1. **Hauled off site** — the contaminated horizon leaves entirely.
2. **Buried under fill or structures** — effectively an unplanned cap.
3. **Stockpiled and reused as landscape/planting medium** — which would relocate the contaminated
   horizon into *precisely* the yards, planters and parkways where children play and vegetables
   are grown.

Option 3 is not exotic; stripped topsoil is commonly retained and reused as the organic-rich
planting layer. **Which of these happened at Ladera Ranch is unknown** and is now an explicit
records request (see §7).

## 3. Exposure pathways — and which ones a developed surface actually blocks

| Pathway | How it works | Does turf/hardscape block it? |
|---|---|---|
| Incidental soil ingestion (hand-to-mouth) | Children ingest soil directly; the dominant pathway in residential risk assessment | **Largely yes** — this is the main protective effect of cover |
| Soil-dust inhalation | Wind erosion of bare soil | **Largely yes** — vegetated/paved surfaces suppress dust generation |
| Dermal contact | Skin contact with soil | **Largely yes** (and dermal absorption of soil arsenic is minor regardless) |
| **House dust** | Outdoor soil migrates indoors on shoes, pets and air; accumulates as an indoor reservoir children ingest | **Only partly** — cover reduces the source term but does not eliminate track-in from edges, slopes, planters and gardens |
| **Gardening / digging** | Any bed, planter, trench, pool excavation or landscape replacement reopens direct contact | **No** — this defeats the cover by definition |
| **Produce uptake** | Arsenic taken up by plants grown in the soil | **No** — cover is irrelevant if the plant roots into the affected layer |
| Disturbance events | Utility work, remodels, re-landscaping, erosion on slopes | **No** — episodic breaches |
| Drinking water | Classic major arsenic route globally | **Not applicable here** — Ladera Ranch is served 100% by imported treated surface water, not local groundwater (`SRC-WS-001`) |

**So the questioner's instinct is right in substance:** paving and sodding does not "solve" soil
arsenic. It closes the largest pathways and leaves a set of smaller, real ones open — chiefly
house dust, gardening, and produce.

### The produce question specifically — and a correction

An initial draft of this assessment understated this pathway. The published work on **vegetables
grown on old orchard sites** (notably McBride and colleagues, Cornell) is more pointed than a
general "metals don't move into plants much":

- **Soil-to-plant transfer is *more* efficient for arsenic than for lead.** Arsenic is not the
  inert case.
- Uptake ranking for arsenic: **lettuce > carrot > bean > tomato**. Root and leafy crops have
  **much higher** potential than fruiting vegetables.
- **Tomatoes can be grown without exceeding health standards even in substantially contaminated
  soil.** Fruiting vegetables are the reassuring end of the range.
- **Leafy vegetables may exceed standards where soil arsenic is elevated**, and **arugula** is
  called out as a particularly strong arsenic accumulator.
- Mechanism differs by metal: most arsenic reaches the plant by **root uptake and translocation
  into above-ground tissue**, whereas most lead on vegetables is **soil particles deposited on
  leaves** (i.e. lead is largely a washing problem; arsenic is not).
- **Compost amendment reduces** both lead and arsenic in leafy vegetables. Phosphate amendments
  reduce extractable lead but can **increase** extractable arsenic — a real trap for a gardener
  trying to remediate.

So the corrected answer to the question as posed: **yes, backyard produce is a genuine arsenic
pathway, and surface cover is irrelevant to it** — but it is strongly crop-dependent. Someone
growing tomatoes and peppers is in a very different position from someone growing lettuce, chard,
arugula and carrots. Grass itself is not a human-intake route (nobody eats the lawn); the produce
concern is specifically about edible gardens.

### Dose is not concentration — and the correction is larger than first stated

Total soil arsenic **substantially overstates** delivered dose. Across **>100 soil samples**, EPA
reports soil-arsenic relative bioavailability (RBA) with a **median of 30%** and a **95th
percentile of 60%**. The commonly quoted "EPA default 0.6" is the *upper percentile*, not the
central estimate.

The directly analogous measurement is better still: at the **Barber Orchard Superfund Site** (a
former lead-arsenate orchard, soils at 280–370 mg/kg arsenic), measured RBA across four soils was
**0.31 ± 0.03**. EPA concluded that assuming default bioavailability **overestimates risk from
those soils by roughly threefold**.

One caution running the other way: arsenic was **enriched in the <250 µm fraction** relative to
whole soil — and that fine fraction is exactly what adheres to hands and gets ingested. Sieved
analysis is the correct method and it cuts against the site.

**Practical consequence:** for former-orchard residential soil, a defensible RBA is ~**0.30**
(measured analogue) to 0.60 (EPA upper bound). Using 1.0 overstates dose by **1.7–3.3×**. Any
sampling should measure **bioavailable** arsenic (IVBA) alongside total.

### EPA soil and dust ingestion rates (children)

| Population | Soil only | Dust only | Combined |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6 weeks – <1 yr | 30 mg/day | — | 60 mg/day |
| 1 to <6 yr (central tendency) | **50 mg/day** | **60 mg/day** | ~100 mg/day |
| 3 to <6 yr (upper percentile) | 200 mg/day | — | — |
| Soil-pica behaviour | 1,000 mg/day | — | — |
| Adult | 20 mg/day | — | — |

*(EPA Exposure Factors Handbook, Ch. 5, Table 5-1, 2011 — grade A1.)* Note that **EPA weights
indoor dust above outdoor soil**, and the IEUBK model apportions **55% dust / 45% soil**. Note
also that EPA folds inhaled-and-swallowed soil particles into these ingestion rates, so inhalation
is not counted separately.

### The house-dust pathway, quantified

This is the pathway surface cover least reliably breaks, and it is the best-evidenced:

- In a **Washington agricultural community** (58 residences), median **house-dust arsenic
  (9.0 µg/g) exceeded median soil arsenic (4.2 µg/g)**; indoor and outdoor levels were
  significantly associated, indicating track-in as an important pathway.
- Measured **soil-to-dust mass transfer coefficients** across eight communities were **0.14–0.47**,
  below EPA's 0.7 default — so roughly 14–47% of indoor dust derives from outdoor soil.
- Intervention data are sobering: the randomised **Safer Yards** project found landscape coverings
  cut lead tracked onto entryway mats by ~50%, yet **entryway floor dust lead did not change
  significantly over a year**. Covering the yard attenuates the source without clearing an
  established indoor reservoir.

## 4. The asymmetry that answers the question directly

This is the cleanest way to hold the two hazards side by side:

> **For arsenic, the turf is a barrier. For turf herbicides, the turf is the source.**

- Arsenic sits *beneath* the grass. Sod, hardscape and structures put distance between a child and
  the contaminated horizon. Cover **reduces** exposure.
- Glufosinate, glyphosate and 2,4-D are applied *onto* the grass, and the treated surface is
  exactly what children then sit, roll and play on. Cover **is** the exposure medium. The dominant
  routes are dermal contact with treated turf, hand-to-mouth after that contact, inhalation during
  and shortly after application, and **track-in of residues into house dust**.

The same landscaping that mitigates one hazard mediates the other. This is why "it's all paved
over" cannot be evaluated without first specifying *which* hazard is being asked about.

| SF Bay RWQCB ESL, shallow residential | Arsenic | 0.39 mg/kg | "nearly always exceeded, even where no anthropogenic contamination has occurred" |

| Washington Dept. of Ecology | Lead | **> 250 ppm** triggers cleanup | |

### The point that reframes everything: background

DTSC analysed **1,086 Southern California school-site samples** (Los Angeles, Orange, Riverside,
San Bernardino, San Diego counties) — directly applicable geography:

| Statistic | Value |
|---|---|
| Mean | **1.51 mg/kg** |
| Median | 1.50 mg/kg |
| Range | 0.15 – 19.63 mg/kg |
| Upper bound UL₀.₉₅(X₀.₉₉) | 11.32 → **rounded to 12 mg/kg** |

US soils generally run a median of ~5.2 mg/kg (USGS, 4,857 sites); ATSDR gives a US mean of
7.2 µg/g.

**So Southern California background sits roughly 18–110× above the risk-based screening levels.**
DTSC states plainly that the risk-based soil concentration is *"100-times below typical ambient
concentrations."* **Exceeding a screening level in Orange County is the norm, not evidence of
contamination.** Any Ladera Ranch soil result must be judged against **12 mg/kg**, not against
0.11 or 0.68 — a distinction that would otherwise manufacture alarm out of ordinary dirt.

Washington's four accepted remedies are instructive about what regulators consider adequate:
**removal**; **hard or soft capping** (asphalt, or soil/grass/gravel) **with ongoing maintenance
and notice to future owners**; **consolidate-and-cap**; and **mixing with clean soil** to dilute
below cleanup levels. Three of the four **leave the contamination on the property**.

Two things follow. First, **capping is genuinely accepted as protective** — a state environmental
agency signs off on grass and gravel as a remedy, which independently validates that surface cover
does real work. Second, it is accepted **conditionally**: on maintenance, on documentation, and on
disclosure to subsequent owners.

**At Ladera Ranch, no soil investigation is known to have been performed. So no remedy was ever
selected, no cap was ever engineered or maintained as a cap, and no disclosure was ever made.**
Whatever protection the turf and hardscape currently provide is **incidental rather than
engineered** — quite possibly substantial in practice, but undocumented and unverified.

## 6. The decisive filter: does arsenic even cause this disease?

Everything above characterises **exposure opportunity**. It does not address whether arsenic
plausibly causes **Ewing sarcoma**, and that question governs whether any of this explains the
reported pattern.

**Inorganic arsenic is an IARC Group 1 human carcinogen — one of the best-established in
toxicology.** The Group 1 classification rests on *sufficient evidence in humans* for cancers of
the **urinary bladder, lung, and (non-melanoma) skin**. In populations with very high
drinking-water arsenic (e.g. Blackfoot-disease regions, ~0.35–1.14 mg/L), elevated risks have
also been reported for **kidney, liver and colon**.

**An important qualifier, stated so as not to overstate the case:** arsenic *is* named in sarcoma
epidemiology reviews as a potential risk factor for **soft-tissue sarcomas**, usually alongside
vinyl chloride and thorium dioxide, and generally in **occupational or high-dose** contexts.
Ewing sarcoma has both osseous and extraosseous (soft-tissue) forms, so this cannot be waved away
as wholly irrelevant.

But the specific claim does not survive contact with the literature: **there is no
well-established association between arsenic exposure and Ewing sarcoma, or with primary bone
cancers generally.** The soft-tissue-sarcoma associations are drawn from occupational exposure
levels, not residential soil concentrations, and they are not Ewing-specific.

Notably, the environmental factor that *does* recur in bone-cancer epidemiology is **parental
farming and residence on a farm**, reported for bone cancers generally and **especially Ewing
sarcoma** — consistent with the farming-proxy studies already in this platform's literature
registry (`LIT-007`, `LIT-008`). One caution against over-reading it here: "residence on a farm"
in those studies means living on an *operating* farm, with active application, livestock and
agricultural dust — not a housing tract built on land that stopped being farmed decades earlier.
The two are not the same exposure.

Two further problems compound this:

- **Latency.** Arsenic-attributable cancers characteristically emerge after **decades** of
  exposure and present in **adults**. The timeframe for producing a bone tumour in a teenager is
  not the timeframe in which arsenic's known carcinogenicity operates.
- **Tissue.** Ewing sarcoma is defined by an **EWSR1-FLI1 gene fusion** arising postnatally for
  reasons that remain unknown, on a strongly **ancestry-patterned germline background**. No
  arsenic mechanism connects to that biology.

**Therefore: even if soil arsenic were confirmed on the footprint, and even if the pathway
analysis above held, it would not specifically explain a cluster of Ewing sarcoma.** It would be
a genuine public-health finding worth acting on for its own sake — arsenic is a serious
carcinogen — but it would be an answer to a different question than the one this community is
asking.

**The same filter disqualifies the pesticide candidates.** Glufosinate is classified by EPA as
*not likely* to be carcinogenic at all. Glyphosate's IARC 2A classification concerns
non-Hodgkin lymphoma. 2,4-D is IARC 2B. **None has an established link to Ewing sarcoma.**

The one candidate in this entire investigation with a *disease-matched* published signal is
**proximity to abandoned oil and gas wells** (Clark et al. 2026: OR 1.27, 95% CI 0.96–1.66 —
suggestive, not statistically significant, and stronger in Hispanic children). That is a weak
signal. It is also the only one pointed at the right disease, which is why the platform's
screening model now carries an explicit **disease-specific plausibility** term.

## 6b. The counterweight — evidence that lowers the prior on arsenic here

Even-handedness requires reporting the evidence that cuts *against* this hypothesis, and it is
substantial:

1. **Biomarker evidence is null at comparable concentrations.** At Middleport, NY, residential
   soil arsenic averaged **19.9 mg/kg** (house dust 11.2 mg/kg) — above New York's 20 mg/kg
   standard at many properties. Speciated urinary inorganic arsenic in children under 7 showed
   **no significant correlation with soil (r = 0.137, p = 0.39) or with house dust (r = 0.049,
   p = 0.73)**. The significant predictor was **rice consumption**. In other words, soil arsenic at
   levels above a state standard did not produce a detectable body burden in children.
2. **Diet dominates.** ATSDR: *"for most individuals diet is the largest source of arsenic
   exposure."* One apportionment puts diet at ~82%, soil and dust ~12%, airborne particulate ~3%.
3. **The childhood-cancer literature does not support the link.** A systematic review of arsenic
   and childhood cancer concluded the literature *"does not seem to support an association between
   arsenic exposure and childhood cancers."* No skin cancers were found among >25,000 children
   examined in high-arsenic areas of southwestern Taiwan and West Bengal. In northern Chile,
   despite very high water arsenic, childhood leukaemia and brain cancer mortality were **not**
   elevated (childhood liver cancer was).
4. **Mass grading probably diluted whatever was there.** Ladera Ranch was mass-graded 1999–2006,
   which cuts, mixes and redistributes the historic 0–6 inch plough layer into engineered fill.
   This typically **dilutes** surface concentrations — while scrambling the spatial signature that
   state orchard programmes rely on. No study quantifies this, and no Ladera Ranch soil data exists
   to test it either way.
5. **Drinking water — the route that carries essentially the entire arsenic toxicity database — is
   not in play.** Every EPA/ATSDR toxicity value for arsenic derives from drinking-water
   epidemiology. Ladera Ranch imports all of its water and uses no local groundwater.
6. **Dermal contact is negligible.** Washington Ecology states arsenic enters the body by ingestion
   or inhalation, *"not through skin contact."* EPA's dermal absorption default of 3% appears to
   overestimate; work on weathered field-aged soils found **<1%**.

## 7. What this changes

**Added to the evidence-gate requests:**

- **Gate G13/G14 (OC Development Services)** — sharpened to ask specifically: was a Phase I ESA or
  Preliminary Endangerment Assessment performed for the residential entitlement? Were **arsenic,
  lead, or organochlorine pesticides** among the analytes? At what **depths**? And **how was
  stripped topsoil handled** — hauled off, buried, or stockpiled and reused as landscape soil?
- **Gate G10 (soil sampling)** — if sampling proceeds, sample the **0–6 inch** horizon in yards,
  parkways and common areas (the child-contact zone), test **total and bioavailable** arsenic
  rather than total alone, and include **indoor house dust**, which is the pathway least affected
  by surface cover.

**Retained as an honest caveat:** we do not know that there is any arsenic on this footprint. It
has never been tested. The neighbouring school-site findings are a reasonable prior — see
`regulatory_asymmetry.md` for why those sites, and only those sites, were ever examined — but a
prior is not a measurement.

## 8. Bottom line

1. Arsenic never degrades; pesticides degrade in days to months. Fundamentally different exposure
   profiles — a permanent low background versus repeated decaying spikes.
2. Surface development **does** meaningfully reduce arsenic exposure — enough that regulators
   accept capping as a remedy — but it does not close house dust, gardening, produce uptake, or
   disturbance events.
3. **Backyard produce is a real pathway and cover is irrelevant to it**, but it is strongly
   crop-dependent: leafy greens and root crops accumulate arsenic; tomatoes and other fruiting
   vegetables largely do not.
4. The surface cuts the other way for turf herbicides, where the treated grass is itself the
   contact surface. **Turf is a barrier for arsenic and a source for herbicides.**
5. **Context is everything on concentration.** Southern California background arsenic runs to
   ~12 mg/kg, roughly 18–110× above the risk-based screening levels. Exceeding a screening level
   here is normal. Only a result above ~12 mg/kg would mean anything.
6. **Bioavailability cuts the estimate further** — ~0.31 measured at the closest analogue site, so
   total soil arsenic overstates dose roughly threefold.
7. **The crop history doesn't match.** Lead arsenate was an apple/pear insecticide. This was
   citrus, barley and cattle country. Any arsenical here more plausibly came from **sodium-arsenite
   herbicide/soil-sterilant** use — a different route that should drive sampling design.
8. **None of this explains Ewing sarcoma.** Arsenic's established cancers are bladder, lung and
   skin, in adults, after decades of exposure. It has some association with soft-tissue sarcomas
   at occupational doses, but none established with Ewing sarcoma or primary bone cancer — and a
   systematic review found the childhood-cancer literature does not support an arsenic association
   at all. The pesticides have no established Ewing link either. **Characterising an exposure is
   not the same as explaining a disease**, and this platform should not let a well-documented
   hazard masquerade as an answer to a question it does not address.

### Revised standing of this hypothesis

Taken together — background swamping the screening levels, ~3× lower bioavailability, a crop
history that doesn't match lead arsenate, mass-grading dilution, imported drinking water, null
biomarker findings at comparable concentrations, and an unsupportive childhood-cancer literature —
**legacy soil arsenic is a LOW-PRIOR hypothesis for this community.**

It is **not excluded**. It has never been measured here, and the cheapest decisive test is direct
**soil plus house-dust sampling** with total and bioavailable fractions. But it should no longer be
carried as the leading environmental explanation, and the platform's screening model has been
adjusted accordingly.

## 9. Verification note

Two of three commissioned research agents for this assessment terminated on API errors before
reporting. The load-bearing claims — arsenic's IARC classification and cancer sites, the absence
of an arsenic/Ewing association, soil bioavailability, and crop-specific uptake — were therefore
**verified directly** rather than accepted on an unreviewed draft. That verification changed two
statements in this document: the produce pathway was **understated** in the first draft and has
been corrected upward, and a blanket "no sarcoma link" was **overstated** and has been qualified
to reflect the soft-tissue-sarcoma literature. Remaining softer areas — house-dust contribution
fractions and California-specific background arsenic ranges — are flagged rather than asserted.
