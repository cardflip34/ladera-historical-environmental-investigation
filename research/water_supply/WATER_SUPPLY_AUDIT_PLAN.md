# Ladera Ranch drinking-water supply — audit, discovery and plan

**Opened 2026-07-31.** Hypothesis-neutral. This audit establishes where the community's water
actually comes from, what it is tested for, and whether any pathway exists by which a local
contaminant could reach taps or irrigation untested. It assumes nothing about what is in any water.

## The questions, stated precisely

| # | Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Q1 | Who supplies Ladera Ranch, and from what sources — imported vs local groundwater vs recycled? | Foundation for everything else |
| Q2 | Is any LOCAL groundwater in the potable system, and from which basin/wells? | A local aquifer is the only route by which ranch-era ground conditions could touch drinking water |
| Q3 | **Is arsenic actually tested in this system?** | The user's core question. Must be answered from regulation AND from the utility's own published results, not assumption |
| Q4 | What explains reported sulfur / rotten-egg odor? | H2S is a signature of REDUCING (anoxic) groundwater — and reducing conditions are exactly the chemistry that mobilizes arsenic from sediments. Odor is not evidence of arsenic, but the two share a geochemical driver, so the reports are worth running down rather than dismissing |
| Q5 | What does RECYCLED water irrigate, and what is it tested for? | Parks and school fields are irrigated with recycled water; children contact that ground. Recycled water is NOT held to drinking-water MCLs |
| Q6 | What monitoring data is already public, and what gaps remain? | Determines whether a records request or new sampling is needed |

## A regulatory fact to state before anything else, because it reframes Q3

**Arsenic is a federally and state-regulated primary drinking-water contaminant.** The US EPA
maximum contaminant level is **10 µg/L (10 ppb)**, adopted 2001, enforceable since 2006 (the prior
standard was 50 ppb). California enforces the same MCL through Title 22, and every community water
system must monitor for it and report detections in its annual Consumer Confidence Report.

So the honest starting frame is NOT "arsenic was never thought to be tested for" — for **potable**
supply, testing is mandatory. The real, sharper questions are:

1. **Detection limit vs reporting limit** — CCRs report against a detection limit for reporting
   (DLR; 2 µg/L for arsenic in CA). "ND" means below that, not zero.
2. **Wellhead vs blended** — compliance samples can be taken after treatment/blending. A local
   well's raw-water arsenic can be higher than what the tap sample shows.
3. **Recycled irrigation water is not a CCR product** — the water on parks and playfields is
   regulated for pathogens (Title 22 recycling criteria), and its trace-metal content is reported
   differently, if at all publicly.
4. **Private/legacy wells and construction water** — water trucked or pumped for 1997–2006 dust
   control and grading was not drinking water and carried no MCL obligation at all.

Those four are where a gap could genuinely live.

# FINDINGS (executed 2026-07-31, live public sources)

## F1 — Where Ladera Ranch tap water actually comes from (Q1, Q2)

**Documented, A2 (utility's own CCR, retained as PDF with text extract):**

> "Your drinking water consists of **imported treated surface water from MWDSC** and treated
> surface water from **IRWD's Baker Water Treatment Plant**, which uses surface water from both
> MWDSC and Santiago Reservoir (Irvine Lake). MWDSC's imported water sources are the **Colorado
> River and the State Water Project**." — SMWD CCR 2026 (2025 data)

And from the district's own Ranch Treatment Plant page: "**Right now, the District imports all of
its drinking water** from hundreds of miles away."

**So the answer to Q2 is: NO local groundwater is currently in Ladera Ranch taps.** The potable
supply is Colorado River + Northern California surface water, treated at MWD's Diemer plant
(Yorba Linda) and IRWD's Baker plant, then distributed by SMWD.

**Where the "groundwater pumped up and treated" story comes from — it is real, but it is the
NEIGHBORING system.** SMWD took over the City of San Juan Capistrano's water system in late 2021.
That system (ID9 — SJC) is documented in its own separate CCR:

> "Your drinking water comes from three sources. **Local groundwater is pumped to the San Juan
> Groundwater Plant, where it is treated by SMWD staff.** Additional water is purchased from IRWD
> and MWDSC." — SMWD CCR 2026, ID9-SJC edition

That is San Juan Capistrano's supply, not Ladera's. Additionally, SMWD's **Ranch Water Filtration
Plant** (Ortega Hwy & Antonio Pkwy, next to the Chiquita Water Reclamation Plant) is the project
that WILL bring San Juan Basin groundwater into the wider supply — target ~20% of district demand
— with the basin **recharged by recycled water**, treated by microfiltration + reverse osmosis +
UV + chloramination. First phase was described as ~2.9 MG/yr. As of the 2026 CCR, the main-system
source statement remains imported-only.

## F2 — Is arsenic tested? (Q3) — YES, and here are the utility's own numbers

Arsenic is a **mandatory** primary drinking-water contaminant (federal MCL 10 ppb since 2006;
California DLR 2 ppb; California PHG 0.004 ppb). It appears in every SMWD CCR examined:

| CCR (data year) | System | Arsenic result |
|---|---|---|
| 2024 (2023) | SMWD main (serves Ladera) | ND, range ND – 2.31 ppb |
| 2025 (2024) | SMWD main — Baker WTP table | avg 2, range 2 – 2.27 ppb |
| 2026 (2025) | SMWD main | ND, range ND – 2.2 ppb |
| 2026 (2025) | **SJC ID9 local groundwater system** | **ND** |

All far below the 10 ppb MCL; no violations. Note all detected values sit above the 0.004 ppb PHG,
which is normal statewide and is why arsenic appears in SMWD's triennial Public Health Goals
reports (2007–2025, all archived on the district site).

**Answer to the user's core question:** if San Juan Basin groundwater enters the potable system —
via the SJC plant now, or the Ranch WFP later — **arsenic testing is not optional. It is required
by federal and state law for every potable source, and the ID9 CCR shows it already being done on
the local groundwater, currently reporting ND.** The scenario "groundwater never thought to be
tested for arsenic" cannot occur in a regulated potable supply.

**Where the question retains real force — three places the mandatory net does NOT cover:**
1. **Recycled irrigation water** (~2.2 billion gal/yr district-wide) on parks, slopes and school
   fields is regulated under Title 22 recycling criteria (pathogens), NOT drinking-water MCLs.
   Its trace-metal content is not CCR-reported. This is gate **G11**, already in the SMWD records
   request (M6-REQ-SMWD-PRA-06).
2. **Raw wellhead vs blended/treated compliance points** — CCR values are for delivered water.
   Raw San Juan Basin water quality is a separate dataset (GAMA / basin authority monitoring).
3. **Construction-era water (1997–2006)** — dust-control and grading water carried no MCL
   obligation at all. What was used and from where is unanswered by any CCR.

## F3 — The sulfur / rotten-egg smell (Q4)

Findings, graded honestly:

- **The 2025 CCR (2024 data) shows an odor excursion at the Baker WTP table: threshold odor number
  range ND – 4 against a secondary standard of 3**, average 2, no violation (secondary standards
  are aesthetic and judged on average). Manganese in the same table ranged ND – 47 ppb against a
  50 ppb secondary standard. *(Documented, A2.)*
- The 2026 CCR's own consumer guidance attributes most rotten-egg odor reports to **household
  causes**: bacteria in sink drains, water heaters, and unmaintained filter systems. *(Official
  statement.)*
- **Hydrogen sulfide from a local aquifer is not a plausible explanation for Ladera tap odor
  today**, because no groundwater is currently in that supply. *(Inference from F1, stated as
  such.)*
- The geochemical point remains worth carrying: H2S odor signals **reducing groundwater**, and
  reducing conditions are the chemistry that mobilizes arsenic from sediments. That is a reason to
  watch the **raw-water** data of any future groundwater source, not a claim about anything now.

## Bottom line, in plain words

Ladera Ranch's drinking water today is imported Colorado River and Northern California surface
water. No local groundwater reaches Ladera taps. Where local groundwater IS used (San Juan
Capistrano's system, run by the same district), arsenic is tested as federal law requires and is
currently non-detect. The reported sulfur smell is most consistent with household plumbing or the
2024 aesthetic excursion at the Baker plant, not a groundwater pathway that does not exist. The
places arsenic monitoring genuinely does NOT reach are recycled irrigation water on the ground
children play on, raw basin water before treatment, and whatever water was used during
construction — and those three, not the tap supply, are where this investigation's effort should
go.

# F5 — CONSTRUCTION ERA (1997–2006): where the drinking water came from

**Question:** during the years Ladera Ranch was graded and built, was tap water imported, or local
groundwater?

**Answer: imported, overwhelmingly — and the small groundwater volume that did exist never went to
Ladera Ranch homes.**

## The primary document

SMWD's **2010 Urban Water Management Plan** (347 pp, downloaded and text-extracted to
`research/water_supply/uwmp/`) carries the year-by-year figures. *(A2 — official agency planning
document, statutorily required.)*

> "**Groundwater** — SMWD has limited access to groundwater supply. SMWD relies on approximately
> **100 acre-feet per year of groundwater from the San Juan Basin to provide service on a contract
> basis to the Nichols Institute.** This local source of supply meets only **0.2% of SMWD's total
> annual demand.** SMWD currently has **one operating well, Well 6**, which is located in the
> **southeast corner of SMWD.**"

## Table 3-6, the district's own historical pumping record

| Fiscal year ending | 2005 | 2006 | 2007 | 2008 | 2009 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| San Juan Basin groundwater (AF) | 90 | 71 | 78 | 65 | 73 |
| **% of total water supply** | **0.3%** | **0.2%** | **0.2%** | **0.2%** | **0.2%** |

Total demand at the time was ~34,200 AF/yr — **28,077 AF imported, 65 AF groundwater, 6,027 AF
recycled.**

## Four findings that settle the construction-era question

1. **The supply was ~99.8% imported.** Groundwater never exceeded 0.3% of district supply in any
   year on record, including FY2005 and FY2006 — squarely inside the construction window.
2. **The groundwater was contractually dedicated to a single non-residential customer.** It served
   **the Nichols Institute** (a laboratory facility in San Juan Capistrano) *on a contract basis*.
   SMWD's groundwater right in the basin is **150 AFY, held under an Agreement with Nichols
   Institute**. It was not general residential supply.
3. **There was one well, and it is in the wrong place.** **Well 6, southeast corner of the
   district** — San Juan Capistrano area, over the San Juan Basin alluvium. Ladera Ranch is
   **upland, north-west of that**, and is not over the producing basin.
4. **Ladera Ranch was built as an imported-water community from the start.** The district's
   facilities serve it from the MWDOC/Metropolitan imported system. The UWMP names Ladera Ranch
   only in the context of service-area boundaries, irrigation demand factors, and weather-based
   controllers — never as a groundwater-served area.

## What this closes, and what it does not

**Closes:** the hypothesis that residents drank local groundwater during 1997–2006 that might have
carried a ranch-era arsenic signature. They did not. The water came from the Colorado River and the
State Water Project, hundreds of miles away, treated at Metropolitan's plants. **A local aquifer
pathway to the tap did not exist during the construction era.**

**Does not close — the genuinely open item:**
**Construction water is a different question from drinking water.** Grading operations use very
large volumes for dust suppression and compaction, and that water carries **no drinking-water MCL
obligation**. It can be non-potable, recycled, or drawn from local sources under a separate permit.
Whether Ladera's 1997–2006 grading used imported potable, recycled, or any locally-sourced water —
and from where — is **not answered by any document reviewed so far**, because construction water
does not appear in a UWMP or a CCR.

That is now the sharpest remaining water question, and it is answerable by records request:
grading permits and SWPPPs (OC Development Services) and construction-water service agreements
(SMWD). Both are already in drafted requests; this finding tells us to make the construction-water
ask **explicit** rather than incidental.

## Note on the 2005 UWMP

The 2005 UWMP (748 pp) is on the district site but is a **scanned image PDF with no text layer** —
0 characters extractable. It has been downloaded and retained. Confirming the FY1997–2004 figures
directly from it would require OCR, and is worth doing if the construction-era supply mix is ever
challenged. The 2010 plan's Table 3-6 already covers FY2005–2009 and shows a stable 0.2–0.3%
groundwater share, so there is no indication of a materially different earlier picture — but that
is an **inference** until the 2005 document is read.
