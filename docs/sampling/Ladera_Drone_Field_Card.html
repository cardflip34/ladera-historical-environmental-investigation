Arroyo Trabuco — Drone Reconnaissance

Ten stations in priority order, what to look for at each, and — just as important — what would rule it out. Built from the 1968 USGS surface-water survey, the 1948 topographic sheet, the 23-target sampling plan, and the USDA vat specification this project already holds.

1 October 2026 · Base imagery: OC Survey 2025 countywide aerial, 1 ft · Hypothesis-neutral: nothing here asserts that a dipping vat exists on this land

Read this before anything else

This document contains locational data and is not for publication. The project's own rule is that no coordinates of the concrete feature or the pond appear on the public site. Keep the geotagged originals off anything public until the staged-disclosure decision is made.

Do not dig, probe or move anything. If the feature is what it might be, the soil around it is the evidence, and disturbing it compromises the sampling that would actually answer the question. Photograph, measure from the air, and leave the ground exactly as you found it. If you handle any soil at all, gloves.

The order of the day

WhenStationsWhy in that order
First light – 08:15D1The low sun is the whole point. Raking light casts shadows into depressions a few centimetres deep and makes a buried footprint legible that is invisible at noon. Nadir grid first, then obliques from all four sides.
08:15 – 10:00D2, D3Still good light. D2 is the historical ranch node; D3 is the slope your find drains across.
MiddayD7, D4, D5, D6Poor light for shadow marks, fine for mapping. Fly nadir grids only and save the obliques.
AfternoonD8, D9, D10Record passes. Drop any of them without regret if batteries are short.
17:00 – sunsetD1 again, then D2The second sun angle is not optional. Shadows fall the opposite way in the evening and features that vanished in the morning reappear. Same altitude, same grid, so the two sets can be compared frame for frame.

D1YOUR AUGUST 2026 CONCRETE FEATURE
—		A concrete rectangle with iron pipe rails still running up to it, in ungraded open space above the community beside the golf course. Floor about 12 ft — the vat bottom specified in USDA Bureau of Animal Industry Circular 183 (1911): "Length at top of vat, 26 feet; bottom, 12 feet."You documented the rim, the rails, a corner and a timber post on foot. What a camera at 200 feet can add is the thing the ground cannot give you: the whole plan form at once. A rectangle in brush is ambiguous. A rectangle with an apron on one end and pen lines funnelling into the other is a dipping station. Fly this first, in the first light of the day, and fly it again in the last.
Caveat: Coordinates are deliberately not held in the project files and are not written here. You know the ground.
D2THE 1948 RANCH STRUCTURE
33.55505, −117.65492		A single building drawn on the 1948 USGS San Juan Capistrano sheet, at a trail convergence on the valley floor beside Trabuco Creek, elevation 307 ft. The map does not label it.This is the only place in the entire study area that the historical record shows as a built ranch working area next to water. Flat ground, water, and several unimproved roads meeting is what a stock-handling site looks like — and that is where a vat would have stood if one stood anywhere here. Look for foundation lines, a pad, regularly spaced post holes, and the ghosts of the converging tracks.
Caveat: Recorded as a ranch-activity node. It is NOT identified as a dip site and a single building is equally consistent with a line camp, a barn, or a ranch house.

What a dipping station looks like from 200 feet

The vat
A narrow rectangle about 8 m long overall. USDA Circular 183 (1911) specifies 26 ft at the top, 12 ft at the bottom. Deep — cattle swam it.
The drip pen
A wider apron at one end, two to four times the vat's width, sloping back toward the vat so the dip drained in and was reused. Usually the largest surviving concrete surface, and the most contaminated ground on the site.
The pens
A funnel of fence lines converging on the entrance, fed by a straight narrow alley. From the air this reads as a V in the vegetation long after the posts are gone.
The cut
Often dug into a low bank so cattle walked in at grade and climbed out a ramp at the far end. Look for a notch in a bank that nothing natural explains.
The water
Within about 30 m. Thousands of gallons per charge; nobody carried it far.
The scar
A bare or stunted vegetation patch that does not match slope or aspect — typically the drip pen and wherever spent dip was dumped. Early October is the best soil-mark season of the year.

And what would rule it out — photograph this just as carefully

The purpose of the flight is to let the plan form decide. That only works if you shoot what is actually there instead of what fits.

  • Shallow and open, with a pipe inlet and no ramp — a water trough.
  • Circular or square pad with anchor bolts and no apron — a tank base.
  • Walls on four sides with a doorway — a building foundation.
  • No ramp, no apron, no pen lines anywhere around it — probably not a dipping station at all.

A well-photographed negative is worth as much as a positive here. It closes a question instead of leaving it open, and this project has more open questions than it needs.

How to shoot it

PassSettings
Nadir mapping grid60 m (200 ft) AGL, 75% front overlap, 65% side. Gives a measurable orthomosaic at roughly 1.5 cm/px — which means you can take real dimensions off it afterwards and test the 26 ft / 12 ft specification against the ground instead of eyeballing it.
Low obliques10–20 m AGL, about 35° down, from all four cardinal directions. Four sides matters: a ramp reads as a ramp from one direction and as nothing from another.
The second sun angleRepeat the D1 grid in the evening at the same altitude and the same track. The comparison is the evidence.
FormatRAW plus JPEG. Geotags ON. Note the clock time of every flight — sun azimuth is what makes the two sets comparable.
ScalePut a 1 m object in at least one frame at D1. Without it the orthomosaic scale rests entirely on GPS.
VideoOne slow orbit at D1 and D2, but stills are the evidence. Do not let video eat the batteries.

What to come back with

  1. A complete nadir set for D1, morning and evening, overlapping enough to build an orthomosaic.
  2. A measurement of the concrete floor that does not depend on memory — the scale object in frame.
  3. How far the iron rails run, and in which direction. They lead somewhere. From the air you can see where.
  4. Whether there is a second concrete surface — the apron is the thing that would most change the reading.
  5. Any bare patch, photographed with enough surrounding ground to judge whether slope and aspect explain it.
  6. The GPS track log, so every frame can be placed later.

Before you launch

Airspace. Check B4UFLY or your LAANC app at the launch point. Do not assume this corridor is uncontrolled. At or below 400 ft AGL, visual line of sight, not over people.

Land. Orange County Parks and the City of Mission Viejo both restrict launching and landing on their open space without a permit. The Arroyo Trabuco Golf Club is private, and the Rancho Mission Viejo land east of the corridor is private. Launch from public right-of-way, or with permission.

Season. It is October. Check for red-flag closures before you drive out, and watch for rattlesnakes and poison oak anywhere you walk in.
