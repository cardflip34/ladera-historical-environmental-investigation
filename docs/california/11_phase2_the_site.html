Phase 2: the site

Summary. In August 2026 a concrete rectangle with iron pipe rails still leading up to it was located on foot, in ungraded open space above Ladera Ranch beside the Arroyo Trabuco golf course. Its floor measures about 12 feet, which is the vat bottom specified for a federal cattle-dipping vat: "Length at top of vat, 26 feet; bottom, 12 feet" [A1, S-USDA-C183, USDA Bureau of Animal Industry Circular 183 (1911) p.11]. It is unfenced, unmarked, and sits beside a public trail. This is a candidate, not an identification. The structure has not been measured to a standard, has not been sampled, and has not been examined by anyone qualified to identify it; the dimensions here are as reported by the observer and are approximate. Nothing here establishes that the structure is a dipping vat, and nothing will until the soil is tested. Phase 1 asked whether California ever looked. Phase 2 found something to look at.

CLASSIFICATION. Structure and setting: field observation (August 2026). Herd arithmetic and mass figures: MODEL ESTIMATE. Florida: [A2], official state record. Imagery and elevation: [A1]. That the structure is a dipping vat remains HYPOTHESIS, and is what a soil test would settle.

11.1 The find

[field observation; field record dated 2026-08-07]

It was found on foot, in August 2026, and the way it was found is the reason a century of aerial imagery missed it. The route ran up onto the high ground above the community, down past the biking and walking trails, and then off them entirely, through unbroken brush and cactus on a slope that was never graded for housing. The structure sits under that cover, in the open space above the community beside the golf course.

That matters for the record, because it resolves an apparent contradiction. The Ladera Ranch chapter records an imagery null, no vat found on adequate imagery, at 1.15 ft/px. That resolution unmistakably resolves a corral in the open. It resolves nothing at all beneath canopy and scrub, and it resolves nothing below grade. The null and the find do not conflict; the null was always a statement about surface facilities visible from the air, and it is now explicitly limited to that.

No coordinates are published here, and none will be: an unfenced structure of unknown chemistry beside a public trail should be closed and marked by an agency, not crowd-visited. What can be described without pinning it:

  • A poured concrete rectangle, its floor roughly 12 ft in length, consistent with the federal specification for a swim-vat floor [A1, S-USDA-C183].
  • Iron pipe rails still in place, running up to the structure, the geometry of an approach chute that funnels animals single-file.
  • No fence, no sign, no marking of any kind.
  • A public trail passes near it, and a rope tire swing hangs on the approach, which is to say the ground around it is in casual recreational use, including by children.

What was measured, and what was inferred. The observed concrete runs about 12 ft, and the precise claim matters. A federal dipping vat is a trapezoid in long section, wide at the rim and narrow at the bottom, and the specification gives both numbers in a single line: "Length at top of vat, 26 feet; bottom, 12 feet" [A1, S-USDA-C183, Circular 183 (1911) p.11, repeated verbatim in Circular 207 (1912) p.14]. Twelve feet is the floor. An observed ~12 ft rectangle sitting at floor level therefore matches the specified vat bottom exactly, with the sloped entry and climb-out that would carry it to 26 ft at the rim either below grade, collapsed, or removed.

The scale longitudinal reconstruction prepared after the visit sets the observed concrete against the specification drawing on one scale and labels the two apart: the 12 ft floor is observed, the full specified length is interpreted, not observed, and the floor itself is below grade and has not been excavated or tested. A reconstruction is an interpretation of what was seen; it is not evidence of what the structure is.

That last point is why this chapter exists. Dip vat or not, an unmarked structure of unknown chemistry in an area of child contact is something an agency should characterise. The position Phase 1 took holds: never looked, not looked-and-cleared. A candidate found by walking is not a finding; it is a place to test.

Safety, before anyone goes looking

The field record carries a warning that belongs on this page too, and it is the reason the location stays unpublished. If this is a dipping vat, the sediment inside the vessel is the single most concentrated place arsenic would be found anywhere in this landscape. That is a different compartment from the soil figures in 11.3: those model the spread across a vat, its pens and its drip pen (128 to 763 mg/kg), whereas sediment sitting in the bath itself is the undiluted end of the range. Where such material has actually been measured, at dip sites in New South Wales and the US Southeast, it runs 500 to 3,000 mg/kg against a California background of 1 to 11 mg/kg [A2, chapter 72]. Accordingly:

  • Do not dig in it, and do not handle or remove the sediment.
  • Do not let children or animals into the structure.
  • Photograph and measure it from outside.
  • Arsenic is an element. It does not degrade, and a century has not reduced it.

This is a precaution attached to an unidentified structure, not a statement that anything is contaminated. Nothing at the site has been sampled, and until it is, the correct posture is distance.

11.2 Why one station is not enough

[MODEL ESTIMATE]

Federal quarantine rules required dipping on a 14-day cadence through the season [A1, S-USDA-C174, 1910]. Against a herd of roughly 21,000 head [A1, S-USDA-C174] to 25,000+ by family accounts of the O'Neill operation [B2, family history], a single vat cannot physically clear the herd on that cycle. Cattle were worked where water was, so the stations followed the ranch's water points. The arithmetic of cadence and herd size implies three to seven vats across the ranch system [MODEL ESTIMATE, confidence low].

One candidate has been found. If the estimate is near right, the others are still out there, unlocated and unmarked, under what is now open space, golf course and neighbourhood. That is an argument for a systematic inventory, the thing Florida did and California never did.

11.3 The arithmetic

[MODEL ESTIMATE, from reports/ARSENIC_QUANTIFICATION_MASTER.md]

Using the published USDA California charge of 8 lb white arsenic (As₂O₃) per 500 gal [A1, S-USDA-C174] and the ranch's herd and cadence:

Quantity MODEL ESTIMATE
Whole-ranch arsenic mass, program lifetime 6,692 – 39,748 lb As₂O₃
Fraction concentrated at a vat, pens and drip pen 1,338 – 7,949 lb
Resulting near-structure soil concentration 128 – 763 mg/kg
California background arsenic, for comparison 1 – 11 mg/kg [A2]

The concentration range is the line that matters: 128–763 mg/kg against a background of 1–11 mg/kg is a point source a standard soil panel would resolve immediately, if anyone ran one. It is also squarely inside the range measured at dip sites in Australia and the US Southeast (chapter 72), which is the empirical anchor for the whole model.

The film states a lethal-dose comparison, and it belongs here with its caveat attached, in full. At a central estimate of roughly 21,000 lb and an adult lethal dose of 100–300 mg, the arithmetic gives 32 to 96 million nominal doses. The caveat is not optional and is reproduced verbatim:

This is arithmetic, not toxicology. Soil-bound arsenic is only partly bioavailable; nobody ingests soil in quantity; the number says one thing: the mass involved was large. It says nothing about exposure, which has never been measured here.

The figure describes the size of the bag, not a dose anyone received. No exposure has been measured in Ladera Ranch, and this project does not claim one occurred.

11.4 Florida: the answer key

[A2, official state record]

Florida ran the same federal program on the same schedule, and then did the thing California never did: it looked.

  • Florida inventoried 3,281 vats statewide.
  • Of twelve sites tested, eleven exceeded the state soil standard.
  • The state's own report records disused vats reused as pesticide-mixing areas, and used by children as swimming holes.
  • Florida fenced and remediated.
  • California has never inventoried a single vat.

Florida is not a hypothesis about what may sit in dip-site soil. It is a measured answer from a comparable program, and the answer was: mostly above standard. The G11 records response from Florida DEP and the photographic exhibit of Florida vats are held with the cross-reference material; note also the Palm Beach gap, a county absent from the state inventory, a reminder that an inventory's silence is not a clearance. See research/national_crossreference/ and research/national_crossreference/EXHIBIT_FL_VAT_PHOTOS.pdf.

11.5 The pond and the drainage

[field observation + A1 imagery + A1 elevation]

Below the find, at the edge of the golf course, is a pond. The imagery record is unambiguous: it is absent in 1929, 1937, 1947, 1953, 1960, 1969, 1980 and 1990, and first appears in the January 2004 orthoimage [A1, OC Survey imagery]. The pond was built with the golf course. It is not a ranch-era feature, and nothing about it is evidence of dipping.

What makes it worth recording is position. On the 2018 county DEM [A1]:

Feature Elevation
Hillside at the find ~421 ft
Pond ~325 ft
Creek ~280 ft

The find sits about a hundred feet above the pond, and the pond sits above the creek. Runoff from that slope passes through the pond on its way to the creek. That is a drainage relationship, not a transport finding: no water, sediment or soil has been sampled anywhere along it.

Separately, a 1968 USGS field survey mapped a 9,111 m² stock-water body on the creek about 240 m upstream (~291 ft) [A1, USGS]. It no longer appears as open water; that corridor is now riparian woodland. A ranch-era stock pond upstream of a modern golf-course pond is exactly the sort of feature an inventory would want mapped.

One observation needs an explicit non-claim: the tule ring around the pond looks dead this season. That has an ordinary seasonal explanation and is not evidence of arsenic damage. It is recorded because it was seen, not because it means anything.

11.6 What has changed since Phase 1

[A2 / factual update]

Two things. Some soil sampling in Ladera Ranch is now under way. What it is testing for, and where the samples are being taken, has not been shared publicly; this project has not been consulted on locations and cannot say whether the dip signature is among the analytes. A sampling programme of unknown scope is progress over none, but it does not answer the question this report poses unless it targets the right places and the right chemistry. Separately, the author is now listed as an Advisory Member (Independent Researcher & Land Historian) at protectladera.org/advisors [A2].

11.7 The ask

Unchanged from Phase 1 in substance, sharpened by having a place to point at.

  1. Close and mark the trail segment at the structure until it has been tested. This is a precaution, not an accusation.
  2. Sample, under hazardous-materials protocol: the base itself; the pens footprint; the drip-pen side; the tire-swing area and trail margin, as the child-contact points; the pond sediment; and the creek margin below it.
  3. Analytes: total and bioavailable arsenic; lead alongside, to separate the dip signature (arsenic trioxide, arsenic with little or no lead) from the orchard signature (lead arsenate); plus speciation.
  4. Independent of Ladera Ranch and of Rancho Mission Viejo, conducted by the State.

A negative result is a real result. If the soil comes back at background, that is an answer this project would publish as readily as any other, and the question closes on evidence rather than on assumption. What is not acceptable is a third decade of neither looking nor clearing.
