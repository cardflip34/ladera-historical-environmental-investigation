Author's note

I'm not a scientist, an epidemiologist, or an activist. I'm a dad in Newport Beach.

I live near the parks, trails, and fields where my kids — and thousands of others across South Orange County — play every day. Like a lot of parents, I started paying attention when I heard about children in our communities being diagnosed with cancer, including Ewing sarcoma. I didn't have a theory. I had a question any parent would have: is there something in the ground, or in what gets sprayed, that we ought to know about — and has anyone actually looked?

By trade I'm a real estate agent, which is really a job about reading land: its history, its records, the paper trail of what a piece of ground used to be before it became what it is. That habit — pulling chains of title, cross-referencing old maps, noticing when a document doesn't quite line up — turned out to be exactly the skill this question needed. I paired it with something new: I used modern AI tools to run deep, patient searches across government databases, historical aerial imagery, century-old newspapers, land-patent archives, and file metadata — surfacing documents that were public but effectively buried, and connecting records that had never been read side by side.

That's how I came across the piece of history at the center of this report: a state-mandated arsenical cattle-dipping program that ran across these ranch lands from about 1907 to 1912, was never mapped, and was never followed up on in the century of development that came after. I did not go looking for arsenic. The land records led me to it.

I want to be clear about what this is and isn't. I have no agenda beyond finding out the truth and keeping our communities safe. This report does not accuse anyone of anything. It does not claim that arsenic — or any pesticide, property, school, or agency — caused any illness. Where the honest answer is "we don't know," I've tried hard to say exactly that, and I've kept experts' own counter-evidence in the report even when it cut against the very thing I was looking into. Especially on the medical questions, I've deferred to the published science rather than to my own hopes about what the data might show. The whole point is to separate what can be documented from what can only be tested — and then to ask the people with the authority and the instruments to do the testing.

I've taken this as far as one person with good tools and a lot of nights can take it. The next steps — pulling the physical ranch archives, and putting a spade and a lab behind the questions the paper record can't close — belong to people and institutions with more reach than I have. I would be genuinely glad to help. I'm happy to share every file, map, and source behind this report, to walk anyone through how it was assembled, and to keep digging alongside anyone who wants to get to the bottom of it.

If you're a researcher, an agency, a journalist, a physician, or just another parent who knows something I don't — I'd like to hear from you. You can reach me, and send documents or leads, through the contact page on this site.
