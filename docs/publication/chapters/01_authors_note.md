# Author's Note — Why This Investigation Began

**By Andy Stavros**

I started looking into the history of this land after learning about the reported pediatric
Ewing sarcoma cases in Ladera Ranch.

**I don't live in Ladera Ranch.** I live in Newport Beach, about twenty miles up the coast, and
I want to be straightforward about that from the outset — this is not my neighborhood and these
are not my neighbors.

What pulled me in was the pesticide discussion that started circulating. Because the moment you
look at what gets applied to maintain a master-planned community — the turf, the parks, the
trails, the common areas — you realize those are the same products, applied by the same kinds of
contractors, in the parks my own kids play in. And in the parks in your neighborhood. And in
every other community across this county built on the same model, on land with the same
agricultural past.

That is what made this feel worth the effort. Not that something happened in one place, but that
if there is a question worth asking about the ground under one master-planned community in
Orange County, it is very likely worth asking about a great many of them.

I am not a scientist. I am not a historian, an epidemiologist, or a regulator. I am a father and
a problem solver. What I had was a question that would not go away, and access to modern AI tools
capable of organizing public records, historical maps, aerial photographs, government reports,
and archival material at a scale I could not have managed alone.

I want to be precise about what this project is, because the subject invites people to hear
something other than what is being said.

**This investigation was not built to prove a theory.** It was built to ask a narrower and
more answerable question: *has every reasonable historical question about this land already
been considered?* When a community is developed on former ranch land, someone is supposed to
look backward — to review the aerial photography, reconstruct what the land was used for, and
identify whether any past activity left something behind. That review happened here. This
project asks how far back it looked, what it found, and whether anything falls outside the
window it examined.

That is a question about the completeness of a historical record. It is not a claim that
anyone was harmed by anything found in it.

**I want to be equally clear about what this project cannot do.** Nothing in these pages
establishes that any pesticide, property, company, agency, school, water provider, or
individual caused any illness. Geographic and temporal overlap is not exposure. Exposure is
not dose. Dose is not causation. Establishing any of those links requires authorized
epidemiological analysis, verified medical information, exposure assessment, and independent
scientific review — none of which this project performs, and none of which it should be read
as substituting for.

There is a particular temptation in work like this, and I have tried hard to resist it. When
you are looking for something, you find patterns. The discipline this project imposes on
itself is that **every claim carries its own counter-evidence**, given the same visual and
editorial weight as the claim itself. Where the evidence is thin, the report says so. Where a
promising line of inquiry collapsed, the report records the collapse rather than quietly
dropping it. Several of the most useful results here are negative ones.

Two examples, both of which cut against the working hypothesis:

The investigation initially concluded that the earliest available aerial photography of this
land was from 1948 — three decades after the cattle-dipping era — and that a dipping vat was
therefore undetectable in principle. That was wrong. Orange County publishes aerial
photography going back to **1929**, and the 1937–38 frames resolve at about 1.15 feet per
pixel. The imagery was searched systematically. **Nothing was found.**

Then I made the opposite mistake. Having found better photographs, I claimed the resolution
problem had disappeared entirely and that a corral complex "would be unmistakable." That was
also wrong. The USDA's own 1911 circular for California describes cage vats costing sixty
dollars, installed beside an existing corral with no drainage pen, and wade tanks costing under
ten dollars in lumber. Neither would necessarily be visible at all.

So the negative result stands as an observation and is now recorded at **low confidence**. I got
it wrong in both directions before getting it right, and both attempts are still in the text.

The second example is more personal. I noticed, looking at property listings, that the homes
here seemed to sit exactly where the old ranch water sources had been — and water is where
cattle gather, and where cattle gather is where they were worked. It felt like a real
pattern. It was tested directly against every water body the 1968 USGS survey mapped, and
against 10,618 building footprints. The measured result: **homes are no more likely to sit on
the old water than on any other ground here.** The pattern I thought I saw was development
covering nearly everything, which is a different thing entirely.

I have left both of those in, in full, because a report that only contains what survived
scrutiny is not an honest report.

What follows is a record of what the public evidence shows, what it does not show, and what
remains genuinely unknown. The unknown category is larger than I would like. I would rather
publish an honest map of my own ignorance than a confident story that later falls apart.

If any part of this is wrong, I want to know, and I will correct it in the open. Corrections
issued so far are listed in the version history at the end, including the two above.
