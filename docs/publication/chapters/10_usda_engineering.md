# USDA Engineering, Poison Warnings, and Dip Designs

The federal government published detailed engineering plans for cattle dipping vats, together
with explicit warnings about the poison they held. Those documents have been obtained and are
reproduced here.

They matter for two reasons. They establish what a dipping installation physically was — which
determines what a contamination footprint would look like. And they contain the detail that
**forced this investigation to downgrade its own central negative finding**.

## 10.2 The poison warning

::: classification fact
**EM-016 · HISTORICAL CONTEXT · Confidence: High**

USDA required a poison notice at every vat. Circular 174 (p. 293) reproduces its text:

> WARNING! The fluid in this vat is POISONOUS to man and all animals.

Circulars 183 and 207 each carry a section headed **"Precautions in the use of arsenic,"**
addressing absorption through cuts in the skin and by vapour, and warning of both livestock and
human fatality.

*Counter-evidence:* This establishes that the practice was federally recognised as hazardous and
was documented as such. It says nothing about whether any vat operated on this land, nor about
the fate of any residue.
:::

@figure FIG-15

The significance is not that arsenic is dangerous — that was never in question. It is that the
federal government **anticipated the hazard, standardised a warning, and published precautions**.
This was a recognised industrial poison handled in bulk in open pits, under official
instruction, across the counties named in Circular 174.

## 10.3 Vat designs

Three designs appear in the California circular, and the differences between them are the
substance of this chapter.

### The swim vat

A long, deep concrete trench — deep enough that cattle swim — with entrance and exit ramps and
holding pens. Circular 174's figure 7 specifies a vat holding approximately **2,088 gallons at
five feet of depth**.

This is a substantial permanent structure. It would leave a large, durable, and photographically
obvious footprint.

@figure FIG-16

### The cage vat

An animal is driven through a short chute into a cage, which its own weight lowers into the
fluid; after twenty to thirty seconds the cage is raised by a rope attached to a saddle horn or
a horse in harness. Circular 174 states such a vat can dip **30 to 50 animals per hour**.

Materials cost **$55 to $65**.

Two details from page 299 are decisive for this investigation:

> This vat is installed near the corrals and requires only a short chute leading from the corral
> to the cage.

and

> Draining pens with such vats are not essential and are rarely used, as the animal drains while
> the cage is being raised.

@figure FIG-17

### The wade tank

> The wade tank is recommended only in the disinfection of very small herds. It can be
> constructed with an outlay of less than $10 for materials, thus being cheaper than a good
> spray pump.

Figure 10 shows a vertical section roughly **fifteen feet long and four feet deep**, with the
dip solution thirty-six inches deep. It could be built "of either lumber or cement."

## 10.4 The correction this forced

::: classification correction
**Correction C-004 — "a corral complex would be unmistakable" was overconfident.**

After obtaining 1.15 ft/px aerial photography, this investigation stated that the resolution
objection to finding a dipping vat had "disappeared entirely," because a vat would be several
pixels across and an accompanying corral complex "would span hundreds of pixels."

That argument assumed the swim-vat model: a large concrete trench with a sloped drip pen and
substantial holding pens. Circular 174 shows this was **not the only California pattern**.

A cage vat cost sixty dollars, was installed beside an *existing* corral, needed only a short
chute, and **had no drip pen**. A wade tank cost under ten dollars in lumber and was fifteen feet
long. Neither necessarily produced any distinctive new surface structure at all — they were
installed against ranch infrastructure that was itself unremarkable.

**Effect:** claim EM-003 is downgraded from Medium to **Low** confidence. The imagery search
would likely have detected a large swim-vat installation and did not. It provides **weak to no
evidence** against a small cage vat or wade tank.
:::

## 10.5 What a small vat means for contamination

Counter-intuitively, a *small* vat is not a smaller contamination problem. It may be a worse one
to find.

**The chemistry is unchanged.** Arsenic is an element. A wade tank charged with arsenical
solution deposits arsenic that does not degrade, exactly as a swim vat does.

**The footprint is smaller and therefore harder to hit.** A fifteen-foot tank contaminates a
patch perhaps ten metres across. A sampling grid designed for field-scale agricultural residue
will pass directly over it and register nothing.

**Disposal was less controlled.** With no drip pen, solution ran off animals directly onto the
ground beside the vat rather than draining back. Where a tank was periodically dumped was a local
decision.

**Nothing survives to find.** A wooden tank in an excavated pit leaves no concrete. It rots or is
removed; the pit is backfilled. Where a swim vat leaves a buried concrete structure that
ground-penetrating radar can detect, a wade tank may leave only disturbed soil and a chemical
signature.

::: classification lead
**This substantially raises the value of soil chemistry relative to geophysics** at candidate
locations. Geophysics detects buried concrete — which a small wooden installation would never
have produced. The arsenic itself is the only durable trace such a facility would leave.

It also sharpens the sampling design: **arsenic and lead together**, at high spatial resolution,
targeted at the immediate vicinity of historical corrals and water — not gridded at field scale.
:::

## 10.5 What these documents do not establish

::: classification open
**None of this places a vat on this land.**

These are national and state-level engineering and instructional documents. They establish what
was built, what it cost, what it held, and that Orange County was in the affected area. They do
not identify a single facility location anywhere in Orange County, and they name no ranch.

The gap between "the programme reached this county" and "a vat stood here" remains entirely
unbridged by documentary evidence.
:::
