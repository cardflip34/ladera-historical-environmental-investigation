LEHRP · California's Forgotten Past · Phase 3

Phase 3 — Written Treatment

One documentary in three connected parts: the concrete structures, where the soil should be tested, and the purple-pipe loop. Complete narration, scene-by-scene storyboard, evidence and citations for every claim, graphics manifest, and the integrated testing plan. Nothing has been rendered. This document is written to be fact-checked before a single frame is made.

Drafted 20 September 2026 · Andy Stavros · Hypothesis-neutral. Asserts no contamination in any soil, runoff, recycled water or irrigation water at any location.

The governing rule of this film

Every factual statement on screen carries a class badge, and the badge is shown to the viewer, not just recorded here.

DOCUMENTED a named document says it, and the document is shown   APPROXIMATE real but located only to within a stated distance   INFERRED reasoned from documents, not stated by them   MODEL output of a calculation with stated parameters   TESTABLE a hypothesis with a named experiment   UNKNOWN nobody has measured it

Three prohibitions hold throughout. No concrete structure is called a dipping vat. No approximate location is drawn as a confirmed one. No contaminant is said to be present in Ladera's soil, runoff, recycled water or irrigation water, because no analytical testing has established that. Where testing has not happened, the absence of testing is the finding.

The question the film asks, in every part: what do the records establish, what don't we know, and where should we test to find out?

A correction this draft makes to earlier project work

Earlier in this investigation I described a concrete structure in San Juan Capistrano as "photographed four days before it was demolished." The primary document does not support "four days," and that phrasing was mine, not the record's. The verified chronology is set out in Part 1, Scene 6. The photograph itself is undated in the report. This correction is made here so the film does not inherit the error.

Part 1 · The concrete structures (0:00–8:30)

The question: where were the possible historical cattle-dipping structures, and what does the record actually say about concrete on this land?

Scene 1 · Cold open 0:00–0:45

In nineteen eleven, the federal government ordered every rancher in this county to build a concrete trough and fill it with arsenic.

A hundred and fourteen years later, a man walking a public trail in Ladera Ranch found a concrete base in the ground.

Nobody has ever tested the soil around it. This film is about why not — and about exactly where you would test, if you wanted to know.

DOCUMENTED the 1907–1912 California quarantine and the arsenical dip requirement — USDA BAI Circular 174, Eradicating Cattle Ticks in California, 1911, which names Orange County among counties "heavily infested." DOCUMENTED the concrete base exists and was photographed. UNKNOWN what it is.

Scene 2 · What a vat is, and what survives of one 0:45–2:15

A dipping vat is a narrow concrete trough, twenty-six feet long and six and a half feet deep, with a sloped entry at one end and a ramp at the other. Beside it sits a drip pen — a flat concrete floor, roughened, sloped toward a drain.

Everything else is timber. The rails the cattle brushed against, the chute gates, the plank walkways. Wood rots. Concrete stays.

This is what one looks like now, in Florida, where the state went looking for them. Low walls under scrub. A trench full of reeds. A ramp half buried. If you did not know, you would walk past it.
Graphics: Circular 207 Fig. 1 animated — plan, then longitudinal section, then cross-section, each labelled as it appears. Cross-dissolve to the 1912 and 1941 federal photographs of vats in use. Then the Florida derelict sequence: overgrown, trench, flooded with ramp, drip-pad slab. Each carries its citation block.

DOCUMENTED dimensions and drip-pen detail — USDA BAI Circular 207, 1912, Fig. 1. DOCUMENTED vats in use — NARA RG 95, NAID 7007707 (27 May 1912, photographer F. Rush) and NAID 2129009 (June 1941). Scene 4 · But the vocabulary exists — for industry 3:40–4:50

Scene 5 · The questionnaire that almost asked 4:50–5:35

There was one question that could have found it. It is printed in every planning area report, and it reads:

Are there currently, or to the best of your knowledge have there been previously, any pits, ponds, or lagoons located on the property in connection with waste treatment or waste disposal?

A vat is a pit. The answer was no.

And I want to be fair about that answer, because the same person, two questions earlier, volunteered something nobody asked them to: years ago we used to dump oil off the corner of the shop building.

That is not somebody hiding things. That is somebody answering honestly a question about waste disposal. A livestock bath is not waste disposal. Nobody was lying. The question simply did not reach.

Part 2 · Where should the soil be tested? (8:30–15:00)

Scene 10 · The land was farmed before it was graded 8:30–10:00

Before you can decide where to dig, you have to know what happened to the ground.

In nineteen thirty-seven this was open rangeland. Natural drainage, no field boundaries, nothing worked.

By nineteen fifty-three, look at it. Contour ploughing across the ridgetops and the valley floors. Curved bands following the slope. Field edges.

And somebody wrote on the photograph. A surname. An acreage — two hundred and thirty acres. A year, nineteen forty-five, and a crop. On the next frame, field numbers circled in ink, and the word beans, over and over.

These aren't just aerial photographs. Somebody used them as farm maps.

And that matters, because ploughing mixes the top foot of soil. It does not remove concrete, and it does not cut the profile.

Constraint the film must respect here

The grading records establish that cut and fill occurred and to what depth. They do not establish the origin of any individual volume of soil. The film must say so in narration, in this scene, in plain words: "The plans tell us how much earth moved and how deep. They do not tell us which earth went where." Any animated soil-movement arrow is therefore INFERRED and must be labelled as a schematic of the grading concept, not a tracked volume. If the grading plans requested in Part 2's records list are obtained and do establish direction, this scene is re-cut.

Scene 12 · Three kinds of ground 11:00–12:00

So by the time anyone could take a sample, this land came in three conditions.

Ground that was ploughed and then graded — the top foot mixed, then the whole profile cut or buried.

Ground that was ploughed but never graded — mixed shallow, but still sitting where it always sat.

And ground that was neither. Never farmed, never cut. That is the ground that still holds its original surface, and it is the most informative ground on the map.

Scene 13 · The twenty-three 12:00–13:40

Earlier in this investigation I mapped every surface water body recorded on this land by the nineteen sixty-eight survey, and asked a simple question of each one: if cattle were dipped here, where would they have been dipped?

Not at the house. Not at the road. At the water. A vat needs several thousand gallons on a fortnightly cycle. You build it where the water is.

That produced twenty-three locations. And then reality intervened, because most of them are now under something.

So each one was re-examined against what is there today — fairway, slope, park, landscape strip, road — and moved to the nearest accessible ground that still relates to the original point.

Scene 14 · Why a control matters more than a hit 13:40–14:40

And here is the part that decides whether any of this means anything.

Arsenic occurs naturally in California soils. A number on its own tells you nothing. A number only means something next to another number.

So every sample at a historic water point is paired — with undisturbed ground that was never farmed and never graded, and with background soil in the same geology, off the ranch entirely.

If the source areas read the same as the controls, that is an answer. A real one. And it would be the most valuable result this investigation could produce.
Graphics: A simple paired-bar animation — source vs control vs background — shown twice: once resolving to "no difference," once to "a difference." Both outcomes shown as legitimate findings, equal weight.

DOCUMENTED arsenic occurs naturally in California soils at variable background levels; control and background sampling is standard practice. TESTABLE the entire question. The narration explicitly frames a null result as a success.

Part 3 · The purple-pipe loop (15:00–23:15)

The water does not end where it drains.

Scene 16 · Two systems, and they are not the same 15:00–16:10

Before anything else, one distinction, because everything downstream depends on it.

Ladera Ranch has two water systems.

One is drinking water. Imported, treated, tested to state standards, published every year in a consumer confidence report. That is not what this part of the film is about, and nothing here should be read as a claim about the tap.

The other is the non-potable system. Recycled water, in purple pipes, for landscape irrigation. Slopes, parks, medians, common-area turf.

Different pipes. Different storage. Different rules. And a very different testing history.

Scene 17 · Where Ladera's runoff actually goes 16:10–18:00

Now follow the water.

Rain and irrigation run off roofs, lawns, streets and slopes. It enters the storm system and the Sienna Botanica corridor, and it drains to Horno Creek.

At the bottom of the watershed it collects in the Horno Water Quality Basin, owned and operated by the County of Orange. When a storm is big enough, the excess carries on toward San Juan Creek and the ocean. The loop takes the low flows — the dry-weather water.

From the basin it passes through seven acres of constructed wetlands owned by the Ladera Ranch Homeowners Association.

And then it is pumped into the district's recycled distribution system, and blended with recycled water, for — in the permit's own words — subsequent landscape irrigation use.

Roughly a hundred and eighty acre-feet a season. About fifty-nine million gallons. Sprayed back onto the community it came from.

Scene 18 · It does not go through the treatment plant 18:00–19:20

There is an assumption worth killing here, because it is the natural one to make.

This water does not pass through the Chiquita Water Reclamation Plant.

The permit proves it four ways, and the cleanest is the monitoring scheme. Provision K sets up two separate sampling points. One: sample the diversion water, either before or after mixing with recycled water. Two: sample the effluent from the recycled water facilities at the point of discharge, prior to blending with diversion water.

Two monitoring points, for two streams, that meet downstream. That is incoherent if the runoff had already been through the plant.

And the waived parameters say the same thing. The diversion water is exempted from biochemical oxygen demand, total suspended solids, and odour — sewage plant metrics. The waiver exists because this stream was never sewage.

So the treatment this water receives, in total, is a settling basin and seven acres of wetland. That is the permitted design. It is normal for urban runoff recovery. What makes it worth a film is the history of the land it drains.

Scene 20 · The correction — what fails and what survives 20:40–21:50

Now I have to correct something, because the obvious story here is wrong and I would rather say so than sell it.

The obvious story is that a loop concentrates. Water goes round, evaporates, comes back stronger, round again — compounding.

It doesn't work. The loop is partial. Storms bypass it. Plants transpire it. Recycled water dilutes it. Model that generously and the enrichment tops out somewhere around one point zero two to one point two eight times. That is not compounding. That is noise.

So the water-concentration story fails.

What survives is different, and simpler. Redistribution — whatever the drainage carries is sprayed across the community rather than leaving it. And deposition — water evaporates, and anything persistent it carried stays in the soil, a little at a time, for two decades and counting.

Those are not the same question. And the second one has never been measured.
Graphics: Two animations side by side. Left, "compounding": concentration climbs then flattens at ~1.1×, clearly labelled MODEL and marked FAILS. Right, "deposition": water arrives, evaporates, a small residue remains, repeat — the residue line rises linearly. Marked TESTABLE.

MODEL recirculation mass balance, enrichment ×1.02–×1.28, transparent parameters — LEHRP Purple-Pipe Loop report v3, 11 Sept 2026, §4 and E8. The film states the failure of its own earlier hypothesis out loud. This is deliberate and must survive the edit.

Scene 21 · Pesticides, handled carefully 21:50–22:30

One more thing to be careful about.

This land grew beans and barley for decades, and it is landscaped intensively now. Both of those are reasons to test for pesticides. Neither is evidence that pesticides are circulating.

So the honest formulation is not "pesticides are in Ladera's water." It is this: given the documented land use, here is the panel a competent laboratory would run, and here is why.

Scene 22 · The water testing design 22:30–23:15

So how would you actually answer it?

Not with one sample. With a network small enough to fund and specific enough to tell sources apart.

You need to know what enters the system, what the basin and the wetlands change, what the recycled stream carries on its own, what comes out of the blend, what reaches a sprinkler head — and what, if anything, ends up in the ground.

Seven points. That is the whole design.

Scene 24 · Closing narration — full text

I want to be clear about what this film has not shown you.

It has not shown you a dipping vat. It has not shown you contamination. It has not shown you a single laboratory result, because there aren't any — and that is the finding, not the failure.

What the records do tell us is where to look. The historical documents tell us what this land was, and what the people surveying it were never asked. The engineering documents tell us where the water goes, and that it comes back. The grading plans tell us the earth was moved, and how deep.

None of that is proof of anything. All of it is a map.

A hundred and fourteen years ago the state told ranchers here to put arsenic in the ground, and they did it because the law required it. Nobody was careless. Nobody was hiding anything. The paperwork simply never had a box for what happened next.

Modern laboratory testing can answer the question the historical record cannot. A sprinkler head. A sediment core. A patch of long-irrigated turf and a patch of ground that was never touched. Soil at a handful of old water points, and soil somewhere that never was one.

That is not an accusation. It is an experiment. And it is a small one.

The records tell us where to look. Someone should look.

Why seven and not eleven

Points considered and not recommended in the first round: basin inlet (W1 and W2 bracket it), wetland inlet as distinct from basin outlet (same water, metres apart), pre-blend diversion water as distinct from W3 (the permit treats these as the same stream), and additional sprinkler locations beyond two or three (diminishing return until a first result exists). Add them only if the first round shows a difference worth resolving.

Access reality. W2, W3, W4 and W5 sit on infrastructure under three-party custody — County OC Public Works (basin), Ladera Ranch HOA (wetlands), Santa Margarita Water District (diversion works and distribution). W1, W6 and W7 are the points most likely to be reachable without an agreement.

E · Sequence, if funding is staged

  1. W6 — sprinkler head. One sample. Cheapest, most direct, answers what is applied to ground people use.
  2. S1 — basin sediment core. The backward-looking sample. Highest information per dollar in the plan.
  3. Long-irrigated soil paired with never-irrigated control. Two samples, one comparison, tests the deposition hypothesis directly.
  4. W3 and W4 — wetland outlet and pre-blend effluent. Separates the two streams. Needs agency cooperation.
  5. Source-area soil at the accessible subset of the 23, each paired with control and background.

What the plan is designed to be able to conclude

That nothing is there. A clean sprinkler sample, a clean basin core and source areas indistinguishable from background would close this question properly, publicly and permanently. The plan is written so that a null result is a real result — which is why every group above has a matched control, and why the film says so out loud in Scene 14.
