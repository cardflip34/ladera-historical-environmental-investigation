LEHRP · Archival method · Phase 2 follow-on

What They Would Have Called It

A concrete dipping vat found during the environmental review of Ladera Ranch would not have been recorded as a dipping vat. It would have been recorded as something else. This report builds the vocabulary of what that something else would be, sweeps 470 pages of the obtained environmental record against it, and follows the trail to the document, the mandated report series, and the physical repository where such a record would actually sit.

Prepared 20 September 2026 · Andy Stavros · Hypothesis-neutral. Establishes no contamination and no finding of a vat at any location.

The premise, stated plainly

This project has already searched the obtained environmental documents for dip vat, cattle dip, sheep dip and dipping, and found nothing. That search assumed the searcher and the original recorder shared a vocabulary. They did not. In 1997 a surveyor walking ranch land who encountered a half-buried concrete trough with iron pipe rails had no reason to know what a 1911 arsenical dipping station looked like. That person still had to write something down. This report asks what they wrote.

What was posted on the vat itself

The California circular reproduces the notice the Bureau required on every vat in the state, verbatim:

WARNING!
The fluid in this vat is POISONOUS to man and all animals.
Do not allow it to contaminate any feed or water supply.
Cattle should be watered before dipping.

The same circular names Orange County among the counties "heavily infested" in the 1911 campaign, alongside San Diego, Santa Barbara, San Luis Obispo, Fresno and Ventura. It records the quarantined area as 69,755 square miles holding 660,027 head of cattle, of which 153,476 were on tick-infested ranges.

It also explains the chemistry. Work began in 1906 with an oil-float dip — one to two inches of California crude floated on water — which proved injurious to stock in the heat. "The following year the arsenical solution was tried in several vats with such satisfactory results to stock owners and inspectors that its general use was at once inaugurated and has been continued uninterruptedly to the present time."

And it describes how small ranches shared the cost: "cooperative vats", where several owners "would club together, select the most convenient location to build the vat, and subscribe the necessary money for its installation," charging about five cents a head. A cooperative vat serves several ranches from one location, which is a reason a vat may sit where no single ranch headquarters stood.

USDA BAI Circular 174, Eradicating Cattle Ticks in California, MacKellar & Hart, 1911. Grade A1.

Part B · What is left of one

Florida has spent thirty years locating, assessing and cleaning up its cattle dipping vats, and its environmental agency has published photographs of what they look like now. This is the answer to the question the lexicon is really asking: what does a person actually see?

Why Florida has these photographs and California does not

Florida ran a state programme. It built a database of vat sites, published cleanup guidance, commissioned assessment reports, and photographed what it found. The photographs above exist because an agency went looking.

No equivalent California programme has been identified. The absence of Californian photographs of derelict vats is not evidence that California has none. It is evidence that no Californian agency has run this exercise.

Part D · Three findings that matter more than a keyword hit

Finding 1 · The questionnaire asked the right question and got a "No"

Every Planning Area Phase I in EIR 589 includes an identical owner questionnaire. One item reads, verbatim:

Q: "Are there currently, or to the best of your knowledge have there been previously, any pits, ponds, or lagoons located on the property in connection with waste treatment or waste disposal?"

This is the closest any standard instrument comes to asking about a dipping vat. A vat is a pit. The answer recorded in every planning area was "No."

Why that answer is unsurprising rather than suspicious. The question is scoped to waste treatment or waste disposal. A rancher in 2003 being asked about waste lagoons has no reason to think of a concrete trough his grandfather's generation filled with arsenic under federal order, decades before he was born, which by then was brush-covered ground. The instrument was answered honestly and it could not have surfaced this. The question failed, not the person answering it.

The same questionnaire block also asks about fill dirt "of an unknown origin." In Planning Area 1 the answer was yes: fill was imported for a lemon grove. Elsewhere, no.

Finding 2 · The concrete vocabulary exists in these documents, but only for industry

Every hit on concrete tank, concrete vault, concrete-lined, concrete pad and concrete slab lands on one of two things: the TRW / Northrop Grumman Capistrano Test Site in Planning Area 8, or facilities along Ortega Highway. Examples: closure of the HEPTS concrete tank; a 500-gallon UST removed from a concrete vault; two concrete-lined containment sumps at the Drum Farm; a laser test pad.

On open ranch land, the recorded language is entirely different. Planning Area 9 is typical: "predominantly vacant, used for grazing and open space… No evidence of contamination, distressed vegetation, petroleum-hydrocarbon staining, waste drums…"

What this shows. The consultants had a precise vocabulary for concrete structures and used it whenever one was attached to an industrial facility. Walking grazing land they applied a different checklist: a petroleum and drum checklist. A derelict concrete trough in brush triggers no item on it. It is not stained. It is not a drum. It is not a tank with a fill pipe. It is invisible to the instrument being used.

Finding 3 · Ranch features were recorded when they were still in use

Corrals appear eleven times. The Oaks Corral received its own dedicated Phase I ESA in July 2002. Windmills are noted individually along Verdugo Canyon and Talega Creek. Historic topographic map review notes "a small corral on the RJO property" from a 1974 sheet.

Why this cuts both ways. It shows the consultants did record ranching infrastructure, carefully, which is a point in their favour. It also sharpens the gap: what they recorded were active, visible, above-ground features — a working horse corral, a standing windmill, a structure legible on a topo sheet. A 1911 vat abandoned by 1912, its timber long rotted, its concrete floor at or below grade under ninety years of brush, is none of those things.

One open lead sits in this material. At Cow Camp, the report states an area "was historically used to bury old equipment and waste scraps," and recommends "that the exact location of the buried debris be identified." Whether that identification was ever performed is not in the documents held. It is a live question for a records request, though Cow Camp is on Ranch Plan land rather than in Ladera Ranch.

Part E · The figures that are not there

This report set out to find images in the environmental record. The most important thing it found about images is an absence, and the absence is documented in the record's own table of contents.

First, how thin the baseline is

Before asking what a 1990s review recorded, it is worth seeing what the first survey of this land recorded. In September 1858 a deputy US surveyor walked the Trabuco Rancho for the General Land Office and produced the plat below. On 22,184 acres he annotated one structure.

None of them is in the document

The 218-page file was examined object by object. It contains nine raster images in total, each 113 by 172 pixels, each in the same position at the head of a Planning Area section. They are the consultant's logo.

What this does and does not mean

It does not mean the photographs were destroyed or withheld. The metadata says this file was scanned on a network copier in March 2004 for distribution. Large-format plates and photo appendices are routinely left out of a scanned circulation copy because they do not feed through a sheet scanner. The likeliest explanation is mundane.

What it does mean is that the photographs exist by reference. A consultant walked this land, took photographs, and bound them into Appendix E of nine separate reports. That is a specific, named, datable body of images, and it is requestable. Until now this project did not know to ask for it.

And it means every conclusion drawn from this document is drawn from its text alone. Anything the consultant recorded as a mark on a site map rather than as a sentence is not in the version that has been read.

The counterpoint, stated up front

A different Phase I, for a parcel inside Ladera Ranch, does contain its site photographs — sixteen of them. They are the subject of Part F. Holding the two documents side by side is the point of this report: one review of this landscape kept its photographs and one did not, and the one that kept them was walking ground that had already been graded flat.

One more thing the text gives up: where the aerials came from

The historical review in each Planning Area states that aerial photographs "dating from 1952 to 1999 were reviewed at Continental Aerial Photo in Los Alamitos, California," with a 2002 frame from EDAW. That sentence appears fourteen times across the nine reports.

It names a commercial aerial photography archive that held frame-level coverage of this land, and it confirms the review window. The compulsory dipping era of 1907 to 1912 is four decades earlier than the earliest frame anyone looked at.

The finding that matters most is in the dates

The environmental professional arrived after the ground was gone

The report states it plainly, in its own words:

"Since before 1942 and up to 1999, the site had been in an undeveloped state and was part of a larger cattle ranch, used for dry farming and grazing. Mass grading of the site began in 1999 and was completed in the summer of 2001."
"At the time of the site reconnaissance, the site existed as a mass graded, leveled lot… Soil covered the entire site. Pavement and/or vegetation were not located within the site."

The site walk happened on 31 January 2002. Grading finished in summer 2001. A Phase I site reconnaissance conducted after mass grading cannot observe anything that stood on the original surface, because the original surface is no longer there. That is not a criticism of the consultant. It is what a Phase I is: a visual assessment of present conditions, performed when the client needs it, which is at purchase, which here was after the developer had already cut the pads.

The report also records the depth involved: "The subject site is underlain with up to 110 feet of soil fill."

The question that was asked of the people who moved the earth

A grading monitor was interviewed. Look at what he was asked.

Section 3.7 of the report records a conversation with the geotechnical firm that supervised the earthwork — Goffman, McCormick & Urban (GMU), represented by Mr Bob Mutchnik:

"A representative of the GMU, Mr Bob Mutchnik, noted that they monitored onsite soil removal and placement activities since grading was initiated in 1999. Aboveground structures were not located onsite prior to the start of grading. Underground structures, such as fuel storage tanks and clarifiers, were not encountered during soil removals. Stained and/or odorous soils were also not encountered during soil removals."

This is the closest thing in the entire evidence set to an eyewitness account of the ground being opened. Somebody watched the earth move from 1999 onward and was asked, three years later, what was in it.

And the question put to him was about fuel storage tanks and clarifiers — the petroleum-and-drums checklist of Part D, finding 2, applied to a cattle ranch. A derelict concrete trough is not a fuel tank and it is not a clarifier. A truthful "no" to the question asked is not an answer to the question this project is asking.

This is not an allegation against anyone. The consultant asked the standard question, and got an honest answer. It is a demonstration of how a specific object can pass through an entire professional process without anybody ever being asked about it.

Where the soil went

Stripped from the surface, and placed lower down

The report's own account of pesticide risk describes a mechanism, and it is worth reading closely:

"Any pesticides and/or herbicides at the site were likely in the upper surficial soils, which were removed prior to fill placement. These materials, if any, were likely mixed with clean soil and/or placed at lower depths during fill placement."

Two things follow, and only two. First, the reasoning is about agricultural spraying on dry-farmed land, which is the right thing to consider for a wheat field and a different question from a point source. Second, and factually: the material is not described as leaving the property. It is described as stripped from the surface, mixed, and placed at depth as engineered fill. The report separately records fill of up to 110 feet, "excavated from onsite sources and from undeveloped areas within the immediate site vicinity."

DTSC's stated concern about this fill, in 2003, was methane from buried organic material. It was resolved by confirming that vegetation was stripped before cut and fill. Arsenic was not the question anyone was asking.

No sample has been taken. Nothing here establishes that anything is in that fill. What it establishes is that if surface soil anywhere in the cut areas carried anything, the documented mechanism moved it down rather than away — and that no one has looked.

Two wells, named in the record

An oil well and a water well, both south of the school site

The same report reviewed historic topographic maps and the Munger Map Book of California oil and gas fields, and recorded this:

"The closest manmade structures shown include a jeep trail and a well (marked as a groundwater well) located approximately 500 feet to the west and 700 feet to the south, respectively." — 1942 Santiago Peak 15-minute quadrangle
"The closest oil well (Union O'Neil 4520121) is shown approximately 750 feet south (down gradient) of the site. This oil well, likely the well shown on the topographic maps, is marked as abandoned."

Why this is worth pulling out. A named, numbered oil well on O'Neil land is a thread that can be followed: California's oil and gas regulator holds well records, and an abandoned well has an abandonment record. It is also the kind of feature that leaves concrete behind — cellars, pads, sumps.

And the water well matters for a different reason. A dipping vat needs several thousand gallons of water on a fortnightly cycle, so vats were built where water was. A groundwater well marked on a 1942 map, 700 feet from a school site, is a location worth knowing about for that reason alone.

Read the report's own hedge. It says the oil well is "likely the well shown on the topographic maps" — treating the 1942 groundwater well and the Munger oil well as probably the same feature. That may be right. It may also be two different wells in the same area, conflated in a desk review. The distinction is unresolved in the record and should not be resolved by assumption.

What the state's own database says about this site

Two entries in the public record, and one absence

The case appears in California's hazardous-site database as EnviroStor 30020004, site code 404477, "Oso Grande School." Its status is "No Action Required," dated 7 October 2003 — the concurrence letter quoted above.

One field in that record is worth reading exactly as written. Against "past use(s) that caused contamination," the entry is:

AGRICULTURAL – LIVESTOCK

Read this carefully, because it is easy to over-read. The site's status is no-action-required, and nothing in the file reports that contamination was found. This is a descriptive land-use classification in a database field, applied to a parcel that was reviewed and cleared. It does not establish that contamination occurred. What it establishes is narrower and still worth recording: when a state agency classified the past use of this piece of Ladera Ranch, the category it chose was livestock agriculture.

Why this correction matters more than any keyword result

A reader could reasonably have assumed this project had searched "the Ladera EIR" and found nothing. It had not. It had searched the Ranch Plan EIR — a different application, a different footprint, approved years later.

Parts C and C therefore describe how the consulting profession wrote about this landscape. They do not describe what was recorded at the specific location in question, because until now the document that would say so could not be named. It can now.

The governing constraint from this project's earlier environmental-review audit still applies: the historical land-use review in the obtained Phase I began at 1952. The 1907–1912 compulsory dipping era sits forty years outside that window. EIR 555 may prove silent for the same structural reason. That is a reason to read it, not a reason to skip it.

What EIR 555 and its appendices should contain

  • A cultural resources technical report — a records search and pedestrian survey of the 4,000-acre plan area. Historic-period ranching features are recorded here, on DPR 523 forms, when they are recorded at all.
  • A biological resources survey, whose field notes routinely describe concrete structures, stock ponds and troughs as incidental observations.
  • A hazardous materials or site assessment section, and any Phase I Environmental Site Assessment prepared for the plan area.
  • Mitigation Measure 26 and its sub-measures A through Q, cited by the Program Text in connection with a Resource and Environmental Management Plan. The full text of that measure is not in any document held.

Part H · The report series that is required to exist

This is the strongest result of the sweep. It is not a keyword hit. It is a condition of approval that obliged someone to be watching the ground while it was opened.

The ARCHAEO/PALEO condition, verbatim

"Prior to the approval of each Area/Subarea Plan or recordation of any final tract/parcel map (except for financing purposes), applicable County EMA Standard Conditions pertaining to archaeological and paleontological surveys, tests, reports, investigations, salvage, excavations and observance of grading activities in compliance with Board of Supervisors, Resolution 77-866 shall be placed on each Area/Subarea Plan or Tract/parcel map…"

Ladera Planned Community Program Text, General Regulations · recovered by OCR for this report

What that sentence commits

  1. It applies to every Area Plan and every tract map in Ladera Ranch. Not to a sample, not to sensitive areas only. Each one.
  2. It includes surveys and reports. A survey produces a report whether or not anything is found, and a negative survey report is still filed.
  3. It includes "observance of grading activities." That is archaeological monitoring: a qualified person physically present while earth is moved. Ladera Ranch was graded through the late 1990s and early 2000s. If a concrete structure was cut through during grading, the person whose job was to notice it was standing there under a recorded condition of approval.
  4. It routes through a named County office — the Manager, Harbors, Beaches and Parks / Program Planning — which means there is an approving authority with a file.

Resolution 77-866, and where the objects went

Orange County Board of Supervisors Resolutions 77-866 and 87-516 require that all County-permitted projects be monitored for archaeological and paleontological materials, and that finds be donated to the County of Orange "for the purpose of promoting scientific study and for display for the education and enjoyment of the people of Orange County."

Those donated materials form the County of Orange Paleontology and Archaeology Collections, now more than six million fossils and artifacts held in perpetuity by the County and curated at a dedicated facility.

The consequence. There are two independent trails, not one. A paper trail of monitoring and survey reports, which goes to the regional CHRIS office. And an object trail of anything collected, which goes to the County collection with an accession record naming the project it came from. A search of the collection by project name is a different question from a search of the literature, and it has not been asked.

Collections contact: copa@ocparks.com · 714-973-6655.

The honest caveat

Archaeological monitoring in the 1990s was, in practice, oriented toward prehistoric deposits and fossils. A monitor trained to watch for lithics and bone, encountering a poured concrete slab of twentieth-century date, would frequently classify it as modern debris outside the scope of the condition and not record it. The condition guarantees that someone was present. It does not guarantee that a concrete feature was written down.

That is still a substantially stronger position than the project held yesterday: from "no record is known to exist" to "a specific, named, mandated report series exists, and it has not been read."

What the document metadata actually showed

Embedded metadata was read for every PDF in the evidence set. It carries no authorship and no geolocation, but it is not empty, and two things in it are worth recording.

  • The Ranch Plan Phase I was scanned on a network copier — creator "Print Server 110," producer "Corel PDF Engine," created 9 March 2004 and modified 9 June 2004. A paper original, digitised for distribution.
  • The four Oso Grande documents were all produced by Adobe's Paper Capture OCR plug-in on 28 April 2011, within about forty minutes of one another. They were digitised as a single batch in one sitting, which is the signature of one request being answered — a useful thing to know when asking an agency what else was in that file.

Where metadata will matter is in material not yet obtained. Native grading-plan CAD files carry coordinates. Original field photographs carry capture dates and sometimes GPS. Both should be named explicitly in any request, because a request for "the report" returns a PDF, and a request for "the associated survey data and field photographs" may return something else entirely.

Part J · What to request, from whom, in what order

Nine requests. Ordered so that each is cheap, independent, and does not wait on the answer to the one before it. Requests 1 and 2 can be filed the same day.

Request 1 · OC Public Works — EIR 555 and its appendices

Name the document by number. Suggested wording:

"Final Environmental Impact Report No. 555 for the Ladera Planned Community, certified by the Board of Supervisors on 17 October 1995, together with Addendum PA970174 approved by the Planning Commission on 7 April 1998, and all technical appendices, specifically: (a) the cultural resources or archaeological technical report; (b) the biological resources technical report; (c) any Phase I Environmental Site Assessment or hazardous materials assessment prepared for the plan area; and (d) the complete text of Mitigation Measure 26, sub-measures A through Q."

Why this wording. A request for "the EIR" routinely returns the certified volume alone. The appendices are where field observation lives, and they are filed separately.

Request 2 · OC Public Works — Area Plans, grading plans, demolition exhibits

"Approved Area Plans and Subarea Plans for the Ladera Planned Community, including the Concept Grading Plan required for each; and rough grading plans with any existing-conditions or demolition exhibit identifying existing improvements to be removed, existing structures, or existing concrete to be demolished, for the tracts comprising Ladera Ranch."

Why this is the strongest public request on the list. It is cheap, it is unambiguously public, and the thing sought appears in it as a labelled drawing with survey coordinates rather than as a sentence somebody had to think to write.

Request 3 · OC Public Works — the complete Appendix I, with its plates and appendices

"The complete Appendix I (Phase I Environmental Site Assessment) to EIR 589, including for each Planning Area report: Plate 1, Figures 1 through 3, and Appendices A through E — specifically Appendix E, Site Photographs. The copy in public circulation contains the report text only; none of the plates, figures or appendices are attached to it."

Why to say the last sentence. Naming the defect in the circulating copy is what stops the request being answered with the same incomplete file. This is the request that could put the consultant's own photographs of this land in front of you.

Request 4 · DTSC — the Oso Grande file, and why the other three schools have none

The Oso Grande case is EnviroStor 30020004, site code 404477, and its four documents are already public and downloadable. Two of them this project does not yet hold:

  • The 8 September 2003 determination that a Preliminary Environmental Assessment was required — three pages.
  • The 2 October 2003 Phase I Update, which appears to recite the 16 September clarification letter and the 30 September site visit. No separate record of either exists as a filed deliverable.

The more important request is the one the database cannot answer:

"For each Capistrano Unified School District school site in Ladera Ranch — Ladera Ranch Elementary, Ladera Ranch Middle School and Chaparral Elementary — please confirm whether any environmental site assessment was prepared, by whom and when, and if no Department review occurred, the statutory basis on which the site was exempt."

Address it to both the district and the department. The expected answer is a procedural one about funding and acquisition dates. Getting it in writing closes the question properly rather than leaving an absence to be misread.

Request 6 · SCCIC records search — through a qualified requester

This cannot be filed as a public-records request and should not be attempted as one. It requires a qualified consultant, an academic researcher, or the landowner. The practical route is to ask whether a faculty member of the CSU Fullerton team already engaged on sampling will submit the records search, since SCCIC sits in their own department.

What to ask for: all previously recorded resources and all previously conducted survey and monitoring reports within the study area and a half-mile buffer, with explicit attention to reports produced under Orange County Board of Supervisors Resolution 77-866 for the Ladera Planned Community, and to historic-period ranching features of any description.

Handle the result carefully. CHRIS records are confidential by statute. Locational data from a records search must not be published, and this project's existing practice of staging coordinate disclosure applies with more force here, not less.

Request 7 · County of Orange Paleontology and Archaeology Collections

A research inquiry to copa@ocparks.com, asking whether the collection holds accession records for material donated from the Ladera Planned Community, and if so, what projects, what dates, and what monitoring firms are named. The accession record is the receipt for the monitoring, and it can establish that a monitor was on the ground even where the report is slow to surface.

Request 8 · Rancho Mission Viejo and the Orange County Archives

Two relationship requests, not legal ones. From the landowner: photographs taken during grading and pre-construction survey of the Ladera villages, naming that original digital files or negatives are preferred over prints, because capture dates and any embedded location data are the point. From the County Archives: Horticultural and Agricultural Commissioner records, Board of Supervisors minutes and photograph collections for 1907–1912 — the only request on this list that targets the era itself.

Request 9 · Ask California whether it has ever done what Florida did

Florida ran a state cattle dipping vat programme: it built a site database, published cleanup guidance, and photographed what it found. Part B is illustrated almost entirely with Florida's pictures because no equivalent California programme has been identified.

Put the question directly to the Department of Toxic Substances Control and the State Water Resources Control Board:

"Has the Department, or any California agency, ever inventoried, assessed or remediated historic cattle dipping vat sites associated with the 1907–1912 tick eradication campaign? If so, please identify the programme and any site list. If not, please confirm that no such inventory exists."

A clear "no such inventory exists" is a useful answer. It converts an assumption in this report into a documented fact, and it is the kind of finding that belongs in the public record whatever else comes of this work.

Limitations, figure provenance, and sources

What this report does not establish

  • It does not establish that a dipping vat exists anywhere in Ladera Ranch. The concrete base observed in Phase 2 is an unidentified concrete structure. No sample has been taken from it and no laboratory result exists for it. No photograph in this report shows it.
  • It does not establish contamination at any location, and it does not establish that any person was exposed to anything. Nothing here shows that anything is present in the fill described in Part F.
  • It does not establish that anything was concealed or that anyone acted improperly. Every gap identified here is explained by the scope of the instrument in use. A questionnaire scoped to waste disposal, a historical review beginning in 1952, a petroleum-and-drums field checklist, a monitoring programme oriented toward prehistoric deposits, and a Phase I lawfully conducted after grading would each independently miss this feature with everyone doing their job correctly. The missing plates of Part E are most likely a scanning convenience.
  • The interview in Part E is not evidence of absence. A grading monitor truthfully answering that no fuel tanks or clarifiers were encountered has answered the question he was asked.
  • The desilting basin photographed in Part E is a modern construction drainage feature, not a historic structure, and is presented as such.
  • Part H establishes that a report series was required to exist, not that it records anything relevant.
  • A zero result is not evidence of absence. It is evidence that the search term and the recorder's vocabulary did not meet.

On publishing locations

Figures in Part F are published at scales that show setting rather than resolving a point location, and the precise site coordinates that appear in the source document are not reproduced. The metes-and-bounds description and the zoning-map course data in Part G are shown as page images and are not transcribed. CHRIS records, if a search in Part I returns any, are confidential under Government Code § 6254.10 and their locational data must not be published at all. This project's practice of staging coordinate disclosure applies throughout.

Method

  • Lexicon. 159 terms in seven registers, built by asking, for each profession that would plausibly encounter the structure, what that profession's standard reporting vocabulary would produce. Register A holds the 22 correct names and functions as a control group.
  • Corpus. Six documents, 470 pages, 956,237 characters. Three were already searchable. Three Oso Grande documents carry 2011 Acrobat Paper Capture OCR. The Ladera Program Text had no text layer and was rendered at 200 dpi and OCR'd for this report using the macOS Vision text recogniser.
  • OCR is imperfect, and this is a real limitation. Roughly half the corpus by page count is machine-read from scans. A term present in the original can fail to match because the recogniser misread a character. A zero result on an OCR'd document is weaker evidence than a zero result on a native text document, and the 0/22 control result should be read with that in mind.
  • Matching. Case-insensitive, with flexible word separators so hyphenated and spaced variants both register. Every non-zero term was read in context to determine what feature and which planning area it referred to. One hit (REC) was discarded as an abbreviation false positive.
  • External archive search. Federal, state and university repositories were searched for photographs of dipping vats and of ranch infrastructure. Every image reproduced here was downloaded and viewed before use, and its rights status checked individually; the provenance table records which are government works, which are Creative Commons and what attribution each requires. Items that could not be verified, and repositories that refused automated access, are named rather than quietly dropped.
  • Photographic-page detection. Scanned pages were additionally classified by tone statistics — midtone mass and ink coverage — to find photographic plates inside image-only PDFs that an object-level scan reports as a single page raster. This is how the sixteen Part E photographs were located. The method over-triggers on aged or densely printed paper, so every candidate was inspected by eye before use.
  • Image survey. Every PDF in the evidence set was walked object by object and each embedded raster measured, then classified by size and by the fraction of the page it covers. Full-page rasters identify scanned pages; small repeated rasters identify logos; anything else is a figure. One-pixel images stretched across a region were identified as Acrobat OCR masking artifacts and excluded. This is how the absence in Part D was established as a fact about the file rather than an impression.
  • Metadata. Embedded PDF metadata was read for every document in the evidence set. Findings are reported in Part H.

Figure provenance

Every figure is an unretouched page render or a crop from a page render of a document in the project evidence set. The photographs in Part E were cropped from four-up scanned plates to show one frame each, with their printed captions intact; they were lightly filtered to suppress the halftone screen of the photocopy and no content was altered. Figure sizes are reduced for the web.
