LEHRP · Evidence extract · Companion to the terminology sweep

What Was Actually Reported

Every passage in the obtained environmental record that describes an observed structure — concrete, corral, post, rail, fence, trough, windmill, well, pit, sump or buried material. Quoted in full, attributed to document and page, and sorted by what the observer said it was.

Prepared 20 September 2026 · Andy Stavros · Hypothesis-neutral. Establishes no dipping vat, no contamination and no exposure at any location.

The answer, first

No. Nobody reported an unknown concrete structure.

Across 288 pages of Phase I Environmental Site Assessment text covering roughly 23,000 acres, the word concrete appears in about thirty observational passages. Every one of them names what the concrete was for. A batch plant. A truck wash-out pit. A containment sump at a rocket test facility. A telecom equipment housing. A curb and gutter. A pile of broken rubble beside a shed. Slabs explicitly attributed to a named former aerospace facility.

There is not one instance, anywhere in the record, of a concrete structure recorded as being of unknown, unstated or unexplained purpose.

The phrases unknown origin and unidentified do appear — nine and five times — and in every case they are the printed wording of the standard owner questionnaire, asking about imported fill dirt and about dumped waste materials. They are never used to describe something a surveyor saw and could not explain.

What this document is

This is the raw evidence behind the terminology sweep. Where that report argues about vocabulary, this one simply prints what the record says. Everything below is a direct quotation. Where a passage is long, it is trimmed at a sentence boundary and marked. Nothing is paraphrased.

Sources: EIR 589 Appendix I, Phase I ESA for Planning Areas 1–9 (Rancho Mission Viejo, 2003, revised 2004); NMG Geotechnical Phase I ESA and 2003 Update for the Ladera Ranch school site; the Ladera Planned Community Program Text. Page numbers are PDF pages.

The one row that comes closest — and why it still is not what we are looking for

Planning Area 7, Cristianitos Canyon, page 165:

"Several concrete slabs and driveways were noted along this central paved road, indicating the former position of structures and facilities at the former Ford Philco facility."

This is the only passage in the entire record describing concrete slabs left behind by structures that are gone. It shows that the consultants did notice orphan concrete and did write it down when they saw it.

But it is immediately explained. The surveyor attributed the slabs to the former Ford Philco aerospace facility — a known, named, twentieth-century industrial operation on that ground. The concrete had a story attached, and the story was correct. Nothing was left unexplained.

Planning Area 7 is Ranch Plan land in Cristianitos Canyon. It is not Ladera Ranch.

Note on the wells, stated carefully

The consultants did review the state oil and gas regulator's files, and found and named two exploration wells on O'Neill land. That is a properly conducted check. Nothing in these passages suggests any of these wells is connected to anything else. They are recorded here because a dipping vat needed several thousand gallons of water on a fortnightly cycle, so water locations are worth knowing — and because the Ladera-specific report separately records a groundwater well and an abandoned oil well roughly 700 to 750 feet south of the school site.

Structures on the 1942 map

The oldest map any consultant reviewed was from 1942. What it showed, in full:

"The 1942 map notes the presence of two small structures located along the ranch access road near the headquarters area, a small structure located south of San Juan Creek in the D&M Nursery area, and four small structures in the current Oaks Corrals area. Ortega Highway is present traversing the subject property, along with San Juan Creek Haul Road and a dirt road south of former Joan Irvine-Smith pasture. No other pertinent items were noted."

Seven small structures on roughly 23,000 acres, none of them described beyond "small structure." A map at that scale cannot show a twenty-six foot trough, and would not label one if it could.

3 · Posts, rails and fencing: the silence

The concrete base observed in Phase 2 has iron pipe rails and timber posts associated with it. That is the most distinctive thing about it. The record has almost nothing to say about such features.

Every fencing observation in 288 pages

Excluding the Ladera Planned Community zoning text, which specifies fence heights and materials for future houses and is not an observation of anything, the complete set is:

  • "…which was cleared and occupied by several trailers and a small fenced area." (p10, PA1)
  • "The O'Connell storage yard was noted, however, no fence was noted." (p63, PA3)
  • "This steel storage building is located in a fenced compound along the southeastern margin of the property." (p165, PA7)
  • "The Drum Farm is a concrete paved, fenced, and covered storage area." (p195, PA8)
  • "12/71; Building Permit; fencing for electric equipment & pump pads-530 L.F." (p114, PA5, permit record)
  • At Ladera Ranch: "Aboveground structures, with the exception of a chain link fence, were not located within the site." · "A chain link fence bordered each side of the site." · "Chain link fencing bordered the entire site." (2003 Update)

That is all of it. Six observations, every one of them modern security or industrial fencing.

What does not appear, anywhere

The record contains no observation of:

  • A timber corral post, a fence post, or a post of any kind standing alone
  • An iron or steel pipe rail, a guide rail, or a railing of any description
  • Derelict, collapsed, weathered or abandoned fencing
  • A loading chute, a squeeze, a headgate or a working pen
  • A stock watering trough, a cistern, or a stock pond
  • A concrete slab, pad or wall on open grazing land

Corrals are named ten times — always as working facilities in current use, with horses in them, on leased parcels with street addresses. Not once is a corral or any part of one described as derelict, historic, or of unknown age.

Why the silence is expected rather than suspicious

Read the field checklist the consultants were applying to open land. It is quoted identically across the ranch planning areas:

"This would include any evidence of contamination, distressed vegetation, petroleum-hydrocarbon staining, waste drums, illegal dumping, or improper waste storage/handling."

Six items. A derelict timber post triggers none of them. It is not contamination, not distressed vegetation, not a petroleum stain, not a drum, not dumping and not waste handling. A person walking with that list in their hand and seeing a rotted post and a length of pipe in the brush has nothing to write it under, and no reason to.

This is the finding in one sentence: the instrument had no field for it.

4 · What the owner was asked, and answered

Every Planning Area report contains the same standard owner questionnaire. It is the one place where a person with direct knowledge of the land was asked, in writing, what had been on it. Here is the relevant part, verbatim.

The respondent was candid, and that matters

The first answer in the sequence is a volunteered admission against interest:

Q: Are you aware of any current or previous uses of the site or adjoining properties which may create an environmental concern?
A: No, with the exception of the maintenance area along Ortega Highway. Years ago we used to dump oil off the corner of the shop building.

Nobody was obliged to say that. It was said anyway. Whatever else this record shows, it does not show a respondent trying to hide things. Other answers disclose drums at the maintenance shop, stained soil at the maintenance shop, and imported fill dirt for a lemon grove.

The closest the instrument ever comes

Q: Are there currently, or to the best of your knowledge have there been previously, any pits, ponds, or lagoons located on the property in connection with waste treatment or waste disposal?
A: No.

A dipping vat is a pit. This is the only question in the entire instrument that could conceivably have surfaced one — and it is scoped to waste treatment or waste disposal. A vat is not a waste facility. It is a treatment bath for livestock. A person reading that question in 2003 has no reason to think of it.

The other questions, and why none of them reach

Nineteen questions. Not one of them asks what this land was used for before living memory. The instrument is built to find a leaking gas station, and it finds leaking gas stations very well.

And what the historical review found for this parcel

"Since before 1942 and up to 1999, the site had been in an undeveloped state and was part of a larger cattle ranch, used for dry farming and grazing. Mass grading of the site began in 1999 and was completed in the summer of 2001."
"There were no known manmade structures located within the site prior to 1999."

The consultant identified the land use correctly — cattle ranch, grazing, dry farming — and classified the parcel as undeveloped. In the vocabulary of this standard, "undeveloped" closes the inquiry. A vat is a structure on land that a surveyor would still call undeveloped.

6 · Two open leads in the record

Passages where the consultants themselves flagged something unresolved and recommended action. Whether that action was ever taken is not in any document held.

Lead 1 · Buried equipment and waste scraps at Cow Camp

From the conclusions of Planning Area 3, listed as a recognised environmental condition:

"Based on conversations with Rancho Mission Viejo personnel, the area east of the Cow Camp maintenance shop area (currently being used to store equipment) was historically used to bury old equipment and waste scraps. EEI recommends that the exact location of the buried debris be identified and excavated, and that soil sampling be performed to determine if any contaminates exist in the pit area or in surrounding areas."

Why it is a lead. It is the only place in the record where somebody with direct knowledge describes material being buried on ranch land, at an unlocated spot, with a recommendation to dig and sample. If old ranch equipment was buried there, old ranch equipment is exactly the category a dismantled dipping station would fall into.

Why it is not evidence of anything. "Old equipment and waste scraps" most plausibly means vehicles, implements and shop waste. The recommendation is about petroleum and metals. And Cow Camp is on Ranch Plan land in Gobernadora Canyon, not in Ladera Ranch.

The open question: was the excavation and sampling ever performed, and what did it find? Ask Orange County and the landowner.

Lead 2 · A 500-gallon tank removed with no record

"Based on conversations with Rancho Mission Viejo personnel, at least one 500-gallon underground fuel tank was removed in the mid 1980's from the Cow Camp area. No information was available with the Orange County Health Care Agency regarding the tank removal, or any soil sampling performed. EEI recommends that the exact location of the former UST be identified, and that confirmation soil sampling be performed."

Why it matters to method rather than to arsenic. Here is a documented case of something being removed from this ranch with no agency record of it at all — known only because a person remembered it and said so in an interview. It is a clean demonstration that the absence of a record does not establish the absence of a thing, on this land, within living memory.

7 · Method and limitations

How this extract was made

  • The full text of the obtained documents was split into sentences and every sentence containing any of thirty structural terms was pulled: concrete, vat, trough, corral, post, rail, fence, windmill, cistern, sump, basin, pit, pond, lagoon, slab, pad, foundation, vault, tank, buried, debris, unknown origin, unknown function, unidentified, well, chute, pen, cesspool, septic, reservoir, structure.
  • That produced 693 candidate passages. Each was read and classified. Boilerplate, table-of-contents lines, regulatory database listings and the zoning code's fence-height rules were set aside as non-observational. The rest are reproduced above.
  • Quotations are verbatim, including the original's typographic errors, which are marked [sic] where they could confuse.

What this extract does not establish

  • It does not establish that a dipping vat exists at Ladera Ranch or anywhere else. No such structure is described in any document quoted here.
  • It does not establish that anyone failed to report something they were required to report. The opposite: the consultants recorded concrete carefully wherever they found it, named ranch features when they were standing, checked the oil and gas regulator's files, and flagged two unresolved items for excavation and sampling. The owner volunteered an admission about dumping oil.
  • It does not establish contamination or exposure anywhere.
  • Absence of a passage is not absence of a thing. Lead 2 above is the proof: a tank was removed from this ranch in the 1980s and no agency holds a record of it.
  • Coverage is uneven. The 218-page Phase I covers the Ranch Plan, not Ladera Ranch. Only about ten acres of Ladera Ranch itself has a site reconnaissance in this record, and that walk happened after the ground had been graded. The environmental review of Ladera Ranch — EIR 555 — has not been obtained.
