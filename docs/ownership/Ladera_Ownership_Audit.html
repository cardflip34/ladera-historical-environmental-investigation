LEHRP · Ownership audit · Companion to Phases 1–3

Who Owned This Land, and Could They Have Known?

The O'Neill–Moiso–Avery line from the 1882 purchase to the present: every generation, every entity, every documented title. Then the question that actually matters for this investigation — whether knowledge of a 1910s arsenical dipping programme could have survived inside the family business — answered from the record rather than assumed.

Prepared 20 September 2026 · Andy Stavros · Hypothesis-neutral. Alleges no wrongdoing by any person or entity named.

What this document is, and what it is not

It is an ownership and control audit. Who held this land, through which entities, under whose management, and when. That is ordinary, necessary work in any environmental land-use investigation, and all of it is public record.

It is not an accusation. Nothing here asserts that any member of the O'Neill, Moiso or Avery family, or any officer of Rancho Mission Viejo, knew of a dipping vat, concealed anything, or acted improperly. No such evidence exists, and the document reaches the opposite conclusion on the central question.

The finding, stated at the top so it cannot be mistaken: the chain of institutional memory that could have carried knowledge of the 1910s dipping programme was broken in 1926, and the break lasted thirty-seven years. The reason is documented, specific, and has nothing to do with anyone's conduct.

One thing does survive, and it is in the company's own words. A history the Santa Margarita Company published in 1992 says that during Jerome O'Neill's management "Nature sent him a host of problems — everything from thistles to ticks." That is the whole of it. No dipping, no vats, no arsenic, no quarantine. Section 6b handles it precisely.

Two corrections to the premises I was given

  1. There is no "Tony Moiso Jr." Rancho Mission Viejo's own biography and every profile located state that Anthony R. Moiso and his wife Melinda have four married daughters. No source names a son or any Moiso child holding an officer title. PREMISE NOT SUPPORTED
  2. The current chief executive is family — by marriage. Jeremy T. Laster, President since October 2022 and promoted to CEO for 2026, is Tony Moiso's son-in-law. That is the succession answer, and it is documented. DOCUMENTED

A third correction, to the company's own published history: Camp Pendleton was taken by federal court condemnation, not purchased. Rancho Mission Viejo's website and several secondary accounts describe it as a sale. The condemnation order is dated 9 July 1942.

Juan (John) Forster, 1814/15 – 20 February 1882

English-born; married Doña Ysidora Pico, sister of Governor Pío Pico, 1837.

Bought the secularised Mission San Juan Capistrano at auction in 1844 for $710. Acquired Trabuco in the 1840s and Rancho Santa Margarita y Las Flores in 1864, by settling the Pico brothers' debts and paying them $14,000. At his peak he held roughly 212,000 to 250,000 acres.

He died insolvent. The 1873 California fencing law forced him to fence the whole holding on borrowed money, and a townsite scheme at San Onofre failed. He left his widow a $207,000 mortgage. That debt is why the land came on the market — and why the O'Neills got it.

1882 — the purchase that begins the modern chain

After Forster's death his heirs sold the combined Santa Margarita, Mission Viejo and Trabuco ranchos. James Clair Flood supplied the entire purchase capital. Richard O'Neill Sr. supplied the cattle expertise and became resident manager.

Title was recorded in Flood's name alone. The arrangement by which O'Neill would work out a half interest was, by every account, a handshake.

Purchase price $457,000, reflecting the assumed $207,000 mortgage. Acreage is reported variously as 200,000, 205,000, 210,000 and 230,000 — no source reconciles it; use "roughly 200,000 to 210,000."

A1 for the 1882 event and the parties — National Register nomination, Santa Margarita Ranch House, 1971. B2 for the terms and the handshake — Camp Pendleton Historical Society, 2013, and Irish America, 1997. No documentary evidence of the handshake was located; the recorded instrument names Flood only.

2 · The family line, five generations

Every person who held or controlled this land, with their documented title and dates. The generation that matters for the dipping question is the second.

Generation 1 — the founder

Richard O'Neill Sr., 17 March 1824 – 7 May 1910

Born County Cork, Ireland. Trained as a butcher in New Brunswick, then Boston; sailed for California about 1850. Married Alice O'Neill (1823–1916).

Titles: resident manager of the ranch from 1882; half owner from 1906 or 1907, when James L. Flood conveyed an undivided half interest honouring his father's handshake after 24 years. Four months later, in declining health, he deeded his half to his son Jerome.
Why he matters here: he ran this ranch as a working cattle operation for 28 years. He was alive and a half owner when the entire county of Orange was placed under cattle-fever quarantine on 1 March 1910. He died two months later.

Conflicts: birthplace given as County Cork by two sources and Dublin by one. Wife's name given as Alice by one source and "Mary, born in Ulster" by another. Half-interest year 1906 or 1907 depending on source.

Generation 2 — the dipping era

Jerome O'Neill, 1861 – 1926

Son of Richard Sr. Disabled by polio.

Titles: owner and operator of the family half interest, 1907–1926. Publicly identified as "owner of the Santa Margarita ranch" in the Times-Advocate, 26 October 1917. In 1923 he and James L. Flood consolidated the partnership into a corporation, the Santa Margarita Company.
This is the man in charge during the compulsory dipping programme. The entire county was quarantined in March 1910 and again in October 1911. The March 1912 proclamation released the north and kept the southern portion, including Township 6 South Range 7 West — the township containing this land. Orange County was still named in a quarantine proclamation in March 1915.

If cattle on this ranch were dipped under state order — and a quarantine of this kind compelled it — Jerome O'Neill was the owner who complied. INFERRED from the quarantine geography; no document names this ranch as a dipping site.

He died in 1926. James L. Flood died the same year. Jerome left a trust dividing his share between his sister Mary Agatha O'Neill Baumgartner and his brother Richard O'Neill Jr.

Richard O'Neill Jr., 28 January 1863 – 28 December 1943

Son of Richard Sr.; brother of Jerome. Married Marguerite "Daisy" Moore in 1916.

Titles: beneficiary of the Jerome O'Neill Trust from 1926. Took the Orange County portion — Mission Viejo and Trabuco — when the ranch was divided around 1939–41. Died days after the Camp Pendleton condemnation judgment period began.

Generation 3 — the matriarch who kept it

Marguerite "Daisy" Moore O'Neill, 1879 – 1981

Widow of Richard O'Neill Jr. Fifth-generation Californian, born Los Angeles; first graduating class of Los Angeles High School, 1898.

Title: none. She held no corporate office. She acted as controlling beneficiary of the trust — which in practice meant she decided.
1944: the bank trust officers attempted to sell the ranch without the beneficiaries' approval on a $1.2 million offer. She refused, and continued operations. Her maxim — "take care of the land and the land will take care of you" — is quoted in the company's own corporate history to this day.

She is the reason the land stayed in the family. She lived to 1981 and saw the first development.

Generation 4 — the development decision

Alice Marguerite O'Neill Moiso Avery, 28 January 1917 – 22 July 2014

Daughter of Richard Jr. and Daisy. Married James Robert Moiso in 1938; divorced about 1944–45. Married Waldo Allard Avery III in 1951. Mother of Tony Moiso, Jerome Moiso and Douglas Avery.

Title: none documented. Consistently described as co-owner, decision-maker and matriarch, never with an officer title.
1963: with her brother Dick, made the decision to open 11,000 acres to development. That decision begins everything this investigation is about.

This marriage is how the Moiso name enters the family. Her first marriage produced the line that runs the company today.

Richard Jerome "Dick" O'Neill, 3 May 1923 – 4 April 2009

Son of Richard Jr. and Daisy; brother of Alice; uncle of Tony Moiso. Cal Poly San Luis Obispo; US Navy, Second World War. Married Donna Newman 1951; no children.

Titles: Board of Directors and general partner, Mission Viejo Company, from 1963. Chairman, Santa Margarita Company — documented as of 1997. Chairman, Rancho Mission Viejo LLC, at his death in 2009. The year he became Chairman of either entity is not documented.

Also Chairman of the Orange County Democratic Central Committee for three terms and State Chair of the California Democratic Party from 1978 or 1979. Bought El Adobe de Capistrano in 1981 — the building formed from the two adobes between which the 1882 stage carrying his grandfather had stopped.

Marguerite Parkway, Alicia Parkway, Avery Parkway and Jeronimo Road are all named for this family.

Donna O'Neill, died 2002

Wife of Richard J. O'Neill from 1951.

Title: none documented. Helped found the land conservancy later renamed in her honour — roughly 1,250 to 1,300 acres, now within The Reserve at Rancho Mission Viejo.

Generation 5 — and 6, by marriage

J. Jerome Moiso, 1941 – 2024  ·  Douglas Avery

Tony Moiso's brother and half-brother.

Titles: both served on the Mission Viejo Company board and are described by the company as continuing owners. No current officer title is documented for either.

Jeremy T. Laster — the succession answer

Son-in-law of Anthony R. Moiso.

Titles: joined Rancho Mission Viejo in 2001 as Project Manager; President from October 2022; promoted to Chief Executive Officer for 2026. Registered agent for 58 entities in the group.

What this table shows, and what it does not

It shows he was the landowner's chief executive at every approval. That is simply what being President for fifty years means, and it is the reason his name belongs in an ownership audit.

It does not show that he had any knowledge of a dipping vat, or that any question about one was ever put to him. No document located places him in the environmental review process personally — the Phase I interviews went to the Ranch Manager, and the consultant was engaged through outside counsel. Section 6 deals with the knowledge question directly.

Two things are now documented that were not before. One is a direct personal connection to the Ladera boundary instrument. The other is an absence.

He is a named grantee on the instrument that defines Ladera Ranch

The Ladera Planned Community Program Text ties its legal description to Parcel C of Lot Line Adjustment LL 95-007, Instrument No. 19950438155, recorded 5 October 1995, 2,390.008 acres.

The Orange County Clerk-Recorder grantor/grantee index for that instrument lists the grantees as:

MOISO ANTHONY R TR · O NEILL RICHARD J TR · SANTA MARGARITA CO

Twelve days before the Board certified EIR 555, Anthony Moiso — as trustee — was a named grantee on the instrument that drew the boundary of Ladera Ranch. A2 index entry, not the instrument text.

The applicant and landowner of record for Ladera was the Santa Margarita Company — the only private party named anywhere in the adopted Program Text, in a condition requiring it to enter an agreement with the City of Mission Viejo. Moiso was its President and Chief Executive Officer, confirmed by his 1993 California Homebuilding Foundation Hall of Fame citation.

Whether he personally testified cannot be determined, and here is exactly why

Orange County's online Board archive covers 2007 to the present. Direct probes of the county agenda system for 1995, 1998 and 2004 all return 404. No statement by Moiso from the 1995–98 Ladera window was located at all. His earliest located Ladera statement is from March 1999, after entitlement.

An attribution trap: there are two Moisos in the Ladera press record

Several 1999–2000 quotations about Ladera Ranch widely attributed to "Moiso" are Anne Marie Moiso, marketing manager of Rancho Mission Viejo, LLC — a different person. Treating her statements as Anthony R. Moiso's would be a factual error, and it is an easy one to make from search results alone. Every "Moiso" quotation in this project must be checked for first name before use.

A second discipline note on the same body of sources. The richest vein of 1999–2004 press quotation is the applicant's own archived website, which republished newspaper articles as re-typed transcriptions carrying obvious corruption — "28,850 acre" for 22,850, "O'Neil family" for O'Neill. latimes.com and ocregister.com were unreachable throughout. Every press quotation sourced that way is B2, medium confidence on exact wording, and must be re-verified against the publisher's own archive before it is published anywhere.

The geography is the point

Township 6 South, Range 7 West is the township containing Ladera Ranch and the former Trabuco and Mission Viejo rancho land. The March 1912 proclamation drew its boundary along the north line of that township and kept everything south of it under quarantine.

So this land was inside the quarantined area from at least March 1910 to at least March 1915 — and a quarantine of this kind compelled systematic arsenical dipping on a fourteen-day cycle.

This corrects our own earlier record, which had the Orange County quarantine ending in March 1912. It did not. The window on this specific land is at least five years long, not three.

What the state records cannot do

The proclamations describe geography, not owners. No individual ranch is named in any of them, and no ranch is named in the State Veterinarian's biennial reports held by this project. A search of those reports for "O'Neill," "Santa Margarita," "Mission Viejo," "Forster" and "Capistrano" returns nothing.

So the documents establish that the O'Neill land sat inside a quarantine that compelled dipping. They do not establish that a vat was built, where it was, or who operated it. The link from quarantine geography to a specific structure remains inferred and untested.

6 · Could they have known?

This is the question you actually asked. The honest answer is no, and the reason is documentable rather than charitable.

The chain of memory breaks in 1926, and stays broken for thirty-seven years

1926. Jerome O'Neill dies. James L. Flood dies the same year. The two men who owned and ran this ranch through the entire quarantine period are gone within months of each other.

1926 to about 1963. The ranch passes into the Jerome O'Neill Trust — a banking trust. For the next thirty-seven years the banks operated the ranch, employing outside managers. The first was Charles Hardy, previously of a San Diego packing house. Not family. Not continuous. Rotating professional employees answering to trust officers.

That is where institutional memory of a 1910s regulatory compliance programme dies. Not through concealment. Through an ordinary generational and managerial discontinuity of a kind that happens to every long-held estate.

The trust was adversarial to the family at least once: in 1944 the bank officers tried to sell the ranch out from under the beneficiaries on a $1.2 million offer, and Daisy O'Neill refused. That is not an arrangement in which operational folklore is lovingly handed down.

And when they were asked, they told the consultants things

Fred Vorhees was Rancho Mission Viejo's designated interviewee for the Phase I environmental work, interviewed repeatedly across planning areas. Eleven distinct disclosures are recorded. He told the consultants:

  • that the land had been used for agricultural purposes and "that pesticides may have been used" — volunteered, more than once
  • that a petroleum pipeline once ran across a site entrance
  • that there was an aboveground diesel tank
  • that part of a property was used for clay mining
  • that there was historically a maintenance shop with underground tanks
  • that a small gasoline tank had been removed in 1984

Separately, the owner questionnaire volunteered: "Years ago we used to dump oil off the corner of the shop building." And the company disclosed that an area at Cow Camp "was historically used to bury old equipment and waste scraps" — which the consultant then flagged as a condition requiring excavation and sampling.

That is a pattern of disclosure, not concealment. The company volunteered information against its own interest on at least three separate occasions in the record.

The answer, stated plainly

Could they have known? On the evidence, no — and there is no indication anyone tried not to.

  1. The people who would have known died in 1926, and the ranch then spent thirty-seven years under bank trusteeship with hired outside managers. The chain broke.
  2. The subject never came up because nobody asked. The questions put to the ranch were about fuel tanks, drums, clarifiers and pesticides — the standard environmental checklist. Nobody asked about a livestock quarantine from before the First World War, because the instrument has no field for it.
  3. What the family did preserve is the founding story — the Flood handshake, the 205,000 acres, Daisy refusing the bank's offer. That is what families preserve. No family history anywhere includes "we complied with a cattle quarantine in 1911." It was a routine legal obligation at the time, universally imposed across the county, and unremarkable.
  4. Your own instinct is the correct one. The dots could not have been passed down, for the same reason they were not passed down anywhere else in the country. This is a gap in the historical record, not a secret.

Nothing in this document supports an allegation against any living person or against the company. If a vat exists, the most likely explanation for why nobody flagged it is the one this whole investigation keeps arriving at: the question was never asked, by anyone, at any stage.

6b · The company's own history mentions ticks

This is the single most significant new finding, and it needs stating with great care.

"Everything from thistles to ticks"

The Santa Margarita Company published a four-page printed history in 1992: "Rancho Mission Viejo — Where History is Still Happening." Describing Jerome O'Neill's management — the exact period of the state quarantine — it says:

"Hard-driving like his father, but tempered by more insight and humor, Jerome brought the ranch new direction. In return, Nature sent him a host of problems — everything from thistles to ticks — while the Depression added such man-made dilemmas as rum-runners and rustlers."

© 1992 Santa Margarita Co. Image-only scan, OCR'd for this project. A2

What this establishes. The company's own published history of the ranch records ticks among the problems of the era in which Jerome O'Neill managed the property. The subject was not unknown to the company in 1992, three years before the Ladera certification.

What it does not establish. It does not say dipping occurred, where any vat stood, what chemicals were used, or that anything of the kind happened inside the Ladera footprint. The publication contains no mention of dipping, vats, arsenic or quarantine. "Ticks" in a list with thistles, rum-runners and rustlers is a colourful sentence about a hard era, not a disclosure.

Two unnamed large ranches in the federal and state record

Neither of these names anyone. Both are recorded here because they are the closest the primary record comes, and because leaving them out would be as dishonest as overstating them.

California State Veterinarian, biennial report to 30 June 1914:

"This work is now confined to one large cattle range in the extreme northern section of San Luis Obispo County, and to one large range in the northern section of San Diego County and extending over the county line into Orange County."

A single large cattle range straddling the San Diego–Orange county line in 1914 is geographically consistent with the Santa Margarita and Mission Viejo holding, which spanned exactly that boundary under one ownership. The report does not name it. INFERENCE ONLY — and it cannot exclude another large operator on that line.

USDA Bureau of Animal Industry, 26th Annual Report, 1909:

"Of the original 153,476 cattle infested there remain under local quarantine 41,906… Half of this number are on one large ranch where eradication work has been slow on account of its size, the roughness of the range, and lack of co-operation from the owner."

This passage names no ranch and no owner anywhere in its 45,000 characters. It is recorded here for completeness. It must not be attached to any family or property without a named source, and none exists. DO NOT ATTRIBUTE

A trap to avoid: there are two Santa Margaritas. A 1910 report of cattle dipped near "Santa Margarita" refers to the Hearst ranch in San Luis Obispo County. Every future hit must be county-checked before it is graded.

The auditable negative

All four California State Veterinarian biennial reports held by this project — 1906, 1908, 1910 and 1912 — were searched for O'Neill, Santa Margarita, Mission Viejo, Flood, Forster and Capistrano. Zero occurrences. Orange County appears only at county level.

Across roughly forty volumes of state and federal reports searched by this project, no source names the O'Neill ranch, Rancho Mission Viejo, Rancho Santa Margarita or any member of the family in connection with tick inspection, quarantine, dipping vats or arsenical dips.

The absence is real in the sources searched. It is not proof that no such record exists — the newspaper databases, which are where owner-level names actually appear, were blocked throughout.

6c · The archives that do exist — and this changes the plan

The company employed a historian for twenty years, and his working papers are public

Jim Sleeper papers, MS-R173, Special Collections and Archives, UC Irvine Libraries. 122.3 linear feet. "The collection is open for research."

From the finding aid, verbatim:

"He served as staff historian for The Irvine Company (1965-1969), and later filled a similar role for the Rancho Mission Viejo Company. He worked as a historical consultant on environmental impact reports, legal cases…"

"1985 Hired as historical consultant for the Rancho Mission Viejo Company; works for them off and on until 2005."

That window spans both entitlements. The finding aid does not say he worked on EIR 555 or EIR 589, and that must not be assumed. What it establishes is that a named historian worked for the company across that period, among other things on environmental impact reports, and his files are open.

A correction to this project's own archival plan

Our records-request plan ranks the Sherman Library in Corona del Mar as the first-visit target, on the belief that it holds O'Neill and Rancho Mission Viejo business records.

A review of Sherman's published archival-collections list found no O'Neill, RMV, Rancho Santa Margarita, Moiso, Avery or Flood collection. An uncatalogued holding cannot be ruled out from a web listing, and a phone call would settle it — but the assumption should not be relied on.

Curiously, Sleeper kept a folder titled "Rancho Mission Viejo: Sherman notes," which may be where the belief originated.

The revised first-visit target is UC Irvine, MS-R173. It is open, catalogued, and 122 feet deep.

And the company has no public archive of its own

No publicly advertised Rancho Mission Viejo corporate or family archive, no published researcher-access policy, no named archivist or curator. The San Juan Capistrano Historical Society's O'Neill Museum holds roughly 6,000 photographs and documents, but has no online catalogue, no finding aid and no posted access policy. Camp Pendleton Base Archives publicly state they hold original documents "from the families who once lived on the Rancho Santa Margarita."

None of that establishes that internal records do not exist. It establishes that none is publicly advertised.

7 · What is unverified, and what to request

Where the family might still hold the answer — and this is the constructive point

The knowledge chain broke in 1926. But paper does not have to remember to survive. A ledger does not need anyone to recall what is in it.

Three bodies of record could still carry the 1910s operational detail, and all three are worth asking for as a cooperative request, not an adversarial one:

  1. The Rancho Mission Viejo corporate and family archive. The company publishes a detailed history, maintains a family cemetery, and Tony Moiso sits on the boards of the San Juan Capistrano Historical Society, the Mission Preservation Foundation and the Camp Pendleton Historical Society. This is a family that keeps things. Ranch ledgers, cattle books and correspondence from the Jerome O'Neill period would answer the dipping question directly.
  2. The Sherman Library, Corona del Mar — already identified in this project as the first-visit target for O'Neill business records.
  3. The Orange County Agricultural Commissioner records at the Orange County Archives. The quarantine was administered locally. Inspection and compliance paperwork was generated locally, and it would name ranches.

Framing matters. The honest ask is: "your ranch was inside a state quarantine that compelled arsenical dipping, nobody today could be expected to know where the vats were, and your own ledgers may be the only surviving record." That is a true statement and an easy one to answer.

What this document does not establish

  • It does not establish that a dipping vat exists on this land, or ever did.
  • It does not establish that any person named here knew of one, concealed one, or had any duty they failed to discharge.
  • It does not establish contamination or exposure anywhere.
  • It does not establish that Rancho Mission Viejo participated in the dipping programme. The land was inside the quarantine area. No document names the ranch.
  • Several officer facts are graded B2, taken from a commercial mirror of the state registry rather than the registry itself, and require primary re-verification.

The central finding is exculpatory, and it should be read that way. The institutional memory that could have carried this forward was extinguished by two deaths in 1926 and thirty-seven years of bank trusteeship. That is a documented historical fact about how estates are managed, not a judgment about anyone's character.
